## Proposed scope (400–550 words)
1. **Definition**: a cognitive bias is a systematic tendency to depart from good reasoning in a predictable direction. Sourced to Tversky and Kahneman (1974) (paraphrase; one short quotation at most).
2. **Heuristics**: biases often come from mental shortcuts that usually work well; this is why biases are systematic, and why everyone has them, experts included (section Q7).
3. **Bias vs fallacy**: a bias is a tendency in the reasoner; a fallacy is a flaw in an argument. A bias can lead to a fallacy (the gambler's fallacy is the standard case; link `gamblers-fallacy`), and a biased reasoner can still give a good argument (consistent with `ad-hominem`).
4. **Three or four examples** (section Q7), one line each with a source: confirmation bias (link), availability, anchoring, possibly the bias blind spot.
5. **Impact on reasoning**: which stage each bias affects: gathering evidence, judging premises, weighing arguments. Reducing its effect: stating the standard form, seeking counterexamples, the principle of charity (Bland pp. 89–90), others' scrutiny. No claim that biases can be eliminated (Bland p. 89).
6. **Questions to explore** (section Q4).
7. **Related**: confirmation-bias, informal-fallacy, gamblers-fallacy, ad-misericordiam, hasty-generalisation, justification-persuasion, misuse-of-argument.
