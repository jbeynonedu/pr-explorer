## Proposed scope (450–600 words)
1. **Definitions**: to justify a claim is to give reasons that show it is right or reasonable to accept (same wording as `purposes-of-argument`); to persuade is to bring someone to accept it, by any means. Persuasion is about the effect on an audience; justification is about the quality of the reasons.
2. **They come apart**: an argument can persuade without justifying (fallacies, emotion, repetition, a likeable speaker) and justify without persuading (a sound argument an audience rejects). Two short constructed examples.
3. **The difference in quality of argument**: a justification is judged by validity/soundness or strength and the truth of premises; persuasiveness is judged by whether it worked. Good arguments can also be persuasive; persuasion is not bad in itself.
4. **Rhetoric**: Aristotle's three modes of persuasion (paraphrased, *Rhetoric* I.2), linked to ethos, pathos, logos; only reasons (logos) bear on justification. Note that emotion is not always irrelevant (link `ad-misericordiam`, "when it is not fallacious").
5. **Plato's distinction**: one short quotation from *Gorgias* on persuasion that produces belief without knowledge versus instruction, with a gloss.
6. **In your own writing**: the syllabus's analytical essay uses "logical rather than rhetorical persuasion" (Designing a course, p. 7, quoted).
7. **Questions to explore** (section Q4).
8. **Related**: purposes-of-argument, argument, valid, sound, strong-weak, ad-misericordiam, ad-populum, ad-verecundiam, straw-man, misuse-of-argument.
