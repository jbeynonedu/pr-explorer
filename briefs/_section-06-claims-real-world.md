## Consistency with approved entries (do not repeat)

- `ad-verecundiam` has the legitimate appeal to authority (expert, field, consensus) and five ways it goes wrong. `credible` links to it and does not repeat the list.
- `ad-hominem`: "A strong interest in the outcome bears on how credible they are, and is a reason to check their claims more carefully. It is not a reason to conclude that the claim is false." `credible` must use the same line (see Q2). `tu-quoque` and `ad-populum` also link to `credible`.
- `purposes-of-argument`: "Justifying is different from persuading: an argument can persuade without justifying its conclusion." `justification-persuasion` develops this; it does not redefine "justify".
- `ad-misericordiam`: "Persuading someone is not the same as giving them good reasons." Its advertisement example (home security, appeal to fear) is taken; `misuse-of-argument` uses a different one.
- `ad-verecundiam` (swimmer and air conditioner) and `ad-populum` ("Australia's favourite family car") also use advertisements. `misuse-of-argument` links to these rather than adding similar ones.
- `informal-fallacy` and `gamblers-fallacy` link to `cognitive-bias`: it explains how a bias (a tendency in the reasoner) differs from a fallacy (a flaw in the argument), and names the gambler's fallacy as a case where the two meet.

- `straw-man` links to `justification-persuasion`; `misuse-of-argument` uses the straw man in political discourse with a link, not a new definition.

## Questions for John
1. **Plausible vs credible.** The syllabus does not define them, and Bland's definition of plausible uses "credible" ("the appearance of being true or credible"). Proposed working distinction, stated once in each entry: a claim is *plausible* if it seems likely to be true given what we already know, judged from its content; it is *credible* if it is worthy of belief given its support, especially the reliability of its source. Both come in degrees; neither means true. The entries would say that ordinary usage and some textbooks use the two words interchangeably.
2. **Source credibility vs "ethos, not logos".** Bland (p. 71) treats a speaker's credibility as ethos, irrelevant to evaluating an argument. The approved `ad-hominem` says interest bears on credibility when you accept a claim on someone's word. Proposed: `credible` keeps both. Source credibility never affects whether an argument is valid or strong. It does affect how much weight to give a claim accepted on testimony, where you cannot check the reasons yourself.
3. **Theories of truth.** Proposed: `true-false` gives correspondence as the working account (as Bland does), plus one or two neutral sentences each on coherence and pragmatic theories, with no verdict. It also covers truth vs belief and certainty, truth vs proof, and how claims are checked (empirical claims vs claims true by meaning). Alternative: correspondence only, with the other two named.
4. **"Generate questions and theses."** Four entries come from this dot point. Proposed: each of `justification-persuasion`, `cognitive-bias`, `confirmation-bias` and `misuse-of-argument` ends with a short "Questions to explore" section: 2–3 open questions that could start an essay or discussion, not quiz questions. This is an exception to "no quick-check questions for now" (style guide §8). Alternative: no question sections.
5. **Length and tabs for `misuse-of-argument`.** Three contexts need room. Proposed: one entry at 900–1,300 words in four tabs (Overview, Political discourse, Advertising, Scientific debate). Style guide §8 limits tabs to argument and theory entries, so this needs your approval. Alternative: three separate short entries, which would change the tracker.
6. **Live issues in examples (style guide §5).** Bland's main real-world examples (minimum wage, climate change, gambling advertising bans, the 2020 US debate) touch live issues. Proposed:
   - political discourse: constructed, paired examples in which both sides of a neutral local issue (e.g. a council parking plan) use the same move;
   - scientific debate: the tobacco industry's 1969 memo on manufacturing doubt (to be verified), a historical and well-documented case; climate change is not used as the illustration;
   - confirmation bias: Lord, Ross and Lepper (1979). Its subject is capital punishment, but it shows supporters and opponents reacting the same way, so it does not take a side. Use it?
7. **Which biases to name.** Proposed: `cognitive-bias` names three or four well-sourced ones only (confirmation bias, availability, anchoring, and possibly the bias blind spot), each with one line and a source. It also explains heuristics (mental shortcuts that are often useful) so bias is not presented as plain stupidity. It gives no long list.
8. **Primary texts in term entries.** Proposed: one short public-domain quotation each where it helps: Hume on testimony in `credible`, Plato's *Gorgias* in `justification-persuasion`, Bacon in `confirmation-bias`. Aristotle's three modes appear in `justification-persuasion` as a paraphrase, linked to Bland's ethos, pathos and logos.
9. **Interactive.** Optional for `confirmation-bias`: a small 2-4-6 task (the reader proposes triples, gets yes/no, then guesses the rule). It shows the bias rather than describing it. Proposed: yes, if Wason's procedure can be sourced exactly.

11. **Drafting order.** Proposed: true-false → plausible → credible → justification-persuasion → cognitive-bias → confirmation-bias → misuse-of-argument (it links to all the others).
