## Proposed scope (300–450 words)
1. **Validity is not truth** (Bland p. 18): a valid argument can have a false conclusion; deduction alone cannot tell you whether premises are true.
2. **Premises often come from induction** (Bland p. 19, Riverview example): many deductive arguments rest on premises established inductively, so their soundness is only as secure as that induction (link to Hume's problem).
3. **No new information** (Bland p. 15 on intermediate conclusions; existing `conclusion` entry): a deductive conclusion only makes explicit what the premises already contain. Gloss carefully: it can still be informative to the reasoner (e.g. a mathematical proof).
4. **Where premises run out**: every argument starts from premises it does not itself prove; deduction cannot establish its own starting points. *Needs a source (e.g. SEP); drop if not sourced.*
5. **Limits at this level**: propositional forms cover only some valid reasoning (e.g. "All... / Some..." reasoning needs other tools). One line, flagged as beyond the course.
6. **Why this matters**: deductive certainty is conditional; knowledge of the world relies heavily on induction (Bland p. 21). No verdict that one is better.
7. **Related**: deduction, valid, sound, deductive vs inductive, induction, Hume's problem of induction, hypothetico-deductive method.
