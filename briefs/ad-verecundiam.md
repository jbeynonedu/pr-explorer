## Proposed scope (350–550 words)
1. **Definition** (Bland pp. 73–74): accepting a claim because someone presented as an authority asserts it, when they are not an authority on that matter, or when the authority is treated as a substitute for reasons.
2. **The legitimate form** (Bland p. 73, set out in `sf`): expert in domain D; claim falls within D; broad agreement among experts in D; therefore the claim is likely true. Inductive, not deductively valid.
3. **How it goes wrong**: no relevant expertise (celebrity endorsement); an expert speaking outside their field; a lone expert against the consensus of their field; an unnamed or vague "experts say".
4. **The pattern** (fallacious): `1. A says P.` / `C. P.` where A lacks relevant expertise.
5. **Examples** (2–3): an advertising endorsement; an expert outside their field (constructed, to avoid naming real people); a "studies show" claim with no source.
6. **When it is not fallacious**: item 2. Relying on genuine expertise is often reasonable because no one can check everything personally (Bland p. 73).
7. **Related**: credible, ad populum, ad hominem (mirror image), inductively strong and weak, justification and persuasion.
