## Proposed scope (350–500 words)
Division of labour: [logical operators](logical-operators) says what each operator means; this entry is the method of getting from English to symbols and back.
1. **Definition**: symbolising replaces each simple proposition with a capital letter (set out in a dictionary) and each connective with an operator (Bland p. 36). Translating also runs the other way, from symbols to English (Bland Exercise 3.1 Q2).
2. **The dictionary**: one letter per simple proposition. Best practice for clarity: state each proposition positively and write negatives with `~` (e.g. `~R` rather than `N = "It is not raining."`). Presented as good practice, not a rule. Keep wording identical across premises (link to the "exact wording" point in modus tollens and standard form).
3. **Cue words** (Bland's chart p. 47, restricted to the four operators): and/but/yet/although → `&`; or/either…or → `∨`; if…then/provided that/whenever/implies → `⊃`.
4. **Tricky cases**, each with a worked example: "P only if Q" → `P ⊃ Q` (link necessary and sufficient); "Q if P" → `P ⊃ Q`; "P unless Q" → `~Q ⊃ P`, equivalent to `P ∨ Q` (Bland p. 47 n. 8; forall x 5.6); "neither P nor Q" → `~(P ∨ Q)` or `~P & ~Q`; "not both P and Q" → `~(P & Q)` or `~P ∨ ~Q`.
5. **Brackets and the main operator**: one three-proposition worked example, building the formula step by step (Bland pp. 44–45).
6. **What symbolising leaves out**: "but", "yet" and "although" all become `&`; the contrast or surprise is lost, only the truth value is kept (Bland p. 38, Brendon example; forall x 10.2, which prefers "symbolise" to "translate" for this reason).
7. **Related**: logical operators, proposition, standard form, necessary and sufficient conditions, the valid forms.
