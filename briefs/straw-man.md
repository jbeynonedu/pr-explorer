## Proposed scope (300–450 words)
1. **Definition** (Bland pp. 74–75): replacing an opponent's actual argument or position with a weaker or more extreme version, then attacking that version as if it were theirs. No Latin name, so English only.
2. **The pattern**: `1. A argues for P.` / `2. [Respondent restates P as P*, a distorted version.]` / `3. P* is false or absurd.` / `C. A's position is wrong.`
3. **Common ways of distorting**: exaggerating to an extreme; oversimplifying; picking the weakest part; attributing views A does not hold.
4. **Examples** (2–3): constructed, school life and everyday disputes. Not Bland's example (it concerns a current federal policy).
5. **When it is not fallacious**: criticising a real but weak version that someone actually holds; drawing out a genuine consequence of A's view (then the question is whether it really follows). The remedy is the principle of charity: restate the strongest version first (Bland p. 75, p. 86).
6. **Related**: ad hominem, slippery slope, informal fallacies, justification and persuasion.
