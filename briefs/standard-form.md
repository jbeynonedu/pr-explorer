# Brief: Standard form (term)

## Syllabus dot points (verbatim)
Unit 1 Topic 1, pp. 11–12:
- Explain how arguments are structured
- Interpret and analyse arguments, ideas and information in order to identify the components of arguments, including premises and conclusions
- Organise information in order to arrange premises and conclusions into standard argument form

Units 2, 3, 4 (every topic): "Organise information in order to arrange premises and conclusions into standard argument form".

Syllabus p. 7: "The structured setting out of this information in standard form is a feature of philosophical analysis. It provides a basis for the evaluation of both the inferential quality of an argument, and the truth of its premises."

## Proposed scope (250–450 (method entry; slightly over the term range) words)
1. **Definition and purpose** (Bland §1.2 p. 13; syllabus p. 7): numbered premises, conclusion last; it separates the two questions evaluation asks (do the premises support the conclusion? are they true?).
2. **Conventions** (house style §3, from Bland): `1.`, `2.`, `C.`, `(HP)`, `(HC)`, `IC1`; one proposition per line; "Therefore" before C.
3. **Method for prose arguments**, as numbered steps: find the conclusion (indicator words, often stated first); find the premises; cut repetition, rhetoric and examples that are not reasons; add hidden premises; reword so each line is a single, complete proposition.
4. **Worked example** from prose to standard form (Penny, Bland p. 13, or a fresh Australian example).
5. **Common mistakes** (Bland §3.7, pp. 68–69): keep philosophers' names out of premises ("Bentham states that..."); keep wording consistent across premises so the links hold; link to dictionary/symbolising.
6. **Related**: argument, premise, conclusion, hidden premise, translating and symbolising, deduction, induction.

Out of scope for this entry (linked instead): symbolising (section 3); evaluation of validity/strength (sections 2, 5).
