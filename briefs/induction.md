## Proposed scope (300–450 words)
1. **Definition by aim** (Bland pp. 19–20): an inductive argument aims to make its conclusion probable, not to guarantee it. Mirrors `deduction`.
2. **Broad and narrow definitions** (Bland p. 20, footnote 3; carried over from the `deductive-vs-inductive` decision, 25 Nov): broad = any argument aiming at probable support; narrow = only recognised patterns (generalisation, analogy, causal inference, statistical syllogism). The Explorer uses the broad definition. Two or three sentences.
3. **Kinds of inductive argument**: generalisation and analogy (linked, one line each); causal reasoning (link `post-hoc`, `cause`) and predictions from past cases. What unites them: premises support without guaranteeing (Bland p. 20).
4. **Worked example**: one argument in an `sf` block (Bland's red-berry example, pp. 20–21, or a fresh Australian one), with a line on why it is inductive.
5. **How it is judged**: strong/weak, in degrees; one line, linking `strong-weak`.
6. **Limitations of induction** (if Q1 = a): fallibility (Bland §5.1 pp. 90–92); a strong argument with true premises can still have a false conclusion (one short example); Hume's problem in two lines, linking `problem-of-induction` and `uniformity-of-nature`.
7. **Related**: deduction, deductive vs inductive, strong-weak, generalisation, analogy, problem of induction, uniformity of nature, hasty generalisation, classifying arguments.

Out of scope (linked instead): the comparison table and "general to specific" correction (`deductive-vs-inductive`); strength criteria (`strong-weak`, `generalisation`, `analogy`).
