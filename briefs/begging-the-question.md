## Proposed scope (350–550 words)
1. **Definition** (Bland p. 82): an argument whose premises assume the truth of the conclusion, often in different words (*petitio principii*). Also called circular reasoning.
2. **The pattern**: `1. P (reworded).` / `C. P.`; and the longer circle where P is supported by Q and Q only by P.
3. **Precision point**: such arguments are usually deductively valid (`P ∴ P` cannot have a true premise and false conclusion; verified by code). The flaw is that they give no independent reason: someone who doubts the conclusion has no reason to accept the premise. So "circular" is not the same as "invalid" (link valid, limits of deduction).
4. **Examples** (2–3): a source trusted because it says it is trustworthy; a definition that relies on the term it defines; a rewording of the conclusion as a premise. Avoid examples that pass a verdict on religious or political claims.
5. **Common mistake**: in everyday speech "begs the question" often means "raises the question"; that is not this fallacy.
6. **When it is not fallacious**: a valid argument's conclusion is always, in a sense, contained in its premises; that alone is not question-begging. The fallacy needs premises that are only acceptable to someone who already accepts the conclusion.
7. **Related**: valid, sound, limits of deduction, hidden premise.
