## Proposed scope (400–600 words)
1. **Definition** (Bland pp. 70–71): rejecting an argument or claim by attacking the person who makes it rather than the argument.
2. **Abusive**: insults or attacks on character. **Circumstantial**: appeal to the person's circumstances, interests or affiliations ("of course you'd say that"). Tu quoque as a third, linked variety.
3. **The pattern** (one schema each): `1. A argues for P.` / `2. A is [abused trait / has circumstance C].` / `C. P is false (or A's argument fails).`
4. **Precision point**: an insult on its own is not the fallacy; the fallacy is using the attack as a reason to reject the claim or argument (parallel to Bland's point that informal fallacies apply to arguments, p. 72).
5. **Examples** (2–3): one real parliamentary example from Hansard (see Q1); a constructed circumstantial example from school life; possibly one from advertising or sport.
6. **When it is not fallacious**: when the person's character or circumstances bear on what is being judged: whether to accept their *testimony* (a witness with a motive to lie), or a conflict of interest that justifies checking their unsupported claims more carefully. This bears on credibility, not on whether their argument is valid or sound (Bland p. 71: ethos versus logos).
7. **Related**: tu quoque, ad verecundiam (the mirror image: accepting a claim because of who said it), credible, justification and persuasion.
