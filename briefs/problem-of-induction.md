  - the limitations of deduction and induction, including David Hume’s problem of induction.

  - the nature, composition and purpose of philosophical arguments about the status of scientific knowledge, including
    - the problem of induction

  - identify the components of arguments, including premises and conclusions
  - identify hidden premises or assumptions
  - classify arguments, e.g. inductive/deductive, argument type

  - the nature of knowledge
  - public scientific literacy/confidence in the context of fallibility.

## Proposed scope (1,000–1,500 words; tabs if longer)
1. **Context**: David Hume (1711–1776); *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40) 1.3.6 and *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) §IV. Relations of ideas vs matters of fact (Hume's fork) in two or three sentences, because the argument depends on it. Dates and first-publication years to source.
2. **Primary text**: extracts from *Enquiry* §IV Part 2 (public domain; may quote at length), each with an "In other words..." gloss. Key passages: the bread/nourishment example; "the future will be conformable to the past"; the circularity point.
3. **Standard form**: one reconstruction, as a deductive dilemma: inferences from observed to unobserved need the uniformity principle; it could be supported only by demonstrative (deductive) or probable (inductive) reasoning; not deductive (its denial is not a contradiction); not inductive without circularity; ∴ inductive inference has no rational justification. Hidden premises marked `(HP)`. Classify: deductive. Validity of the symbolised skeleton verified by code.
4. **Argument map** (cobalt, Reason branch).
4a. **The uniformity of nature** (merged section; John, 26 Nov 2026; ~200 words): what the principle says (Bland p. 90; "the uniformity principle"); Hume's own wording (*Enquiry* §IV, *Treatise* 1.3.6, copied from fetched text); its role as a hidden premise: one inductive argument in an `sf` block, then with the principle added as `(HP)` (validity of any symbolised version checked by code); a precision note that the principle is usually put as observed regularities continuing, if SEP supports it.
5. **Hume's own response**: custom or habit (*Enquiry* §V): a psychological explanation of why we reason inductively, not a justification (Bland p. 91). Hume does not conclude we should stop.
6. **Objections and replies** (even-handed, each with a reply; 3–4 only):
   - Inductive justification ("induction has worked so far") and the circularity reply.
   - Ordinary-language / dissolution reply (Strawson: being reasonable just *is* proportioning belief to inductive evidence), with the reply that this changes the subject.
   - Pragmatic vindication (Reichenbach: if any method works, induction will), with its limits.
   - Popper: give up justifying induction; science proceeds by deduction and falsification. One paragraph, linking Section 8 (section note Q4).
   Sources: SEP Henderson (paraphrased). Strawson is in copyright: short quotes only, if any. Reichenbach's public-domain status under Australian rules depends on his date of death: confirm at Sourced before quoting.
7. **How to evaluate it**: validity of the reconstruction, then premise by premise; where the dispute lies (whether justification must be deductive or inductive; whether "justification" is the right demand). No verdict.
8. **Illustration**: Russell's chicken (*The Problems of Philosophy*, 1912, ch. VI). Russell d. 1970, in copyright: paraphrase or one short quote.
9. **Questions it raises**: knowledge; public confidence in science given fallibility (link Section 8). Neutral.
10. **Related and sources**: induction, strong-weak, limits of deduction, deductive vs inductive, induction in science, H-D method, falsification.

Not in scope (proposed): Goodman's "new riddle" (grue); Bayesian responses beyond one sentence, if any.
