## Proposed scope (300–500 words)
1. **Definition**: from a disjunction and the denial of one disjunct, conclude the other disjunct; the "process of elimination" (Bland pp. 28–29).
2. **The form**: `P ∨ Q, ~P ∴ Q`; standard form beside symbolic form. Also `P ∨ Q, ~Q ∴ P` (either disjunct may be denied).
3. **Example**: fresh Australian example with dictionary.
4. **Why it is valid**: interactive four-row truth table (widget to be extended for `∨`).
5. **Variations**: more than two disjuncts, eliminated one at a time (Bland's Cluedo example, p. 29); negated disjuncts, e.g. `~P ∨ Q, P ∴ Q`.
6. **Look-alike forms**: affirming a disjunct, `P ∨ Q, P ∴ ~Q` (invalid, because `∨` is inclusive: both may be true). Counterexample row checked by code. When the "or" really is exclusive ("but not both"), the extra premise `~(P & Q)` makes that inference valid; it is then a different argument.
7. **Where it matters**: the argument is only as good as its first premise. If the disjunction leaves out an option, the argument is valid but unsound. Sherlock Holmes's "when you have eliminated the impossible..." (Arthur Conan Doyle, *The Sign of the Four*, 1890; Conan Doyle died 1930: public domain) as the classic statement, with that caution.
8. **Related and sources.**
