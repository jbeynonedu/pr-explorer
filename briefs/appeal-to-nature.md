## Proposed scope (300–500 words)
1. **Definition** (Bland p. 74): inferring that something is good because it is natural, or bad because it is unnatural, with no further support. Latin *ad naturam* as subtitle (syllabus has no Latin name; Bland uses *argumentum ad naturam*).
2. **The pattern**: `1. X is natural.` / `C. X is good.` (and the "unnatural, so bad" version). Show the hidden premise "whatever is natural is good" and why it fails (counterexamples both ways, Bland p. 74).
3. **"Natural" is ambiguous**: occurring without human involvement; normal or usual; in accord with a thing's nature. The fallacy often trades on sliding between them.
4. **Examples** (2–3): food or health-product marketing; a "natural remedy is safer" health claim; an everyday moral judgement. Constructed unless a clean sourced instance is found.
5. **When it is not fallacious**: when "natural" stands in for a specific, relevant property that is supported by evidence; and arguing that some natural things have value is a legitimate philosophical position (Bland p. 74). The fallacy is the bare inference, not the topic.
6. **Distinguish** (one line each): the is-ought fallacy; G. E. Moore's "naturalistic fallacy" (different idea despite the name; see section Q5).
7. **Related**: is-ought fallacy, descriptive and normative claims, hidden premise, value, good.
