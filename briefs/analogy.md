## Proposed scope (400–550 words)
1. **Analogy and argument from analogy** (Bland §5.2 pp. 94–95): an analogy is a comparison, often to explain (familiar source → unfamiliar target); an argument from analogy uses the comparison to conclude that one thing probably has a further property. Not every analogy is an argument (link `purposes-of-argument`).
2. **The pattern**: same letters as `faulty-analogy`: `1. A and B are alike in respects r1, r2, r3...` / `2. A has property Q.` / `C. Therefore, B probably has property Q.`
3. **Criteria for strength** (Bland pp. 94–95): truth, relevance, number, diversity, disanalogy. This is the full list that `faulty-analogy` defers to; relevance and disanalogy weigh most (to confirm against a second source before saying so).
4. **Worked evaluation**: one constructed example (school, sport or consumer), assessed against each criterion.
5. **Famous example**: Paley's watchmaker (William Paley; dates as given in Bland p. 95, to confirm at Sourced) named in one or two sentences and linked to `teleological-argument`, with no evaluation here (contested; evaluated in the religion section).
6. **In science** (if section Q3 approved): one paragraph, one sourced example (e.g. animal models in medical research, or Darwin's comparison of artificial and natural selection: source whichever is fetchable).
7. **Related**: induction, strong-weak, generalisation, faulty analogy, purposes of argument, teleological argument.
