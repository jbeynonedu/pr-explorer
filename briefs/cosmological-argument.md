## Proposed scope (1,500–2,200 words, in tabs)
1. **The argument (tab)**: what cosmological arguments share: a posteriori, from the existence of the universe (or of change, causes, contingent things) to a first or necessary cause. Three versions:
   - Aquinas, *ST* I q. 2 a. 3, the first three Ways (motion, efficient cause, contingency), quoted from a PD translation with glosses. Note that Aquinas's first two Ways are usually read as ruling out an infinite regress of causes *now* acting, not of causes in time (sourced; SEP).
   - Kalam: whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; so it has a cause. Origins in Islamic philosophy (al-Kindī, al-Ghazālī; section Q8); modern defender William Lane Craig (paraphrase).
   - Leibniz: the principle of sufficient reason; why is there something rather than nothing? (*Monadology* §§32–38 or "Ultimate Origination", PD).
2. **Standard form and map (tab)**: kalam (short, deductive; modus ponens / syllogism skeleton checked by code) and the argument from contingency, with `(HP)`s. Classify both as deductive. Argument map for the contingency version.
3. **Objections and replies (tab)**:
   - What caused God? — reply (the argument is about things that begin to exist / contingent things).
   - Why not an infinite regress? — reply (per se vs per accidens series; arguments against an actual infinite).
   - Composition: every part has a cause, so the whole does? (link `composition-division`; Hume *Dialogues* IX; Russell–Copleston, paraphrased) — reply (the argument does not rely on that inference).
   - Is the first cause God? (the "gap" from first cause to the God of theism) — reply (further arguments; the attributes of a necessary being).
   - The principle of sufficient reason may be false; quantum events — reply.
4. **Evaluating it (tab)**: the forms are valid; the dispute is over the causal principle, regress, and the gap. Link `cause` (Section 8). No verdict.
5. **Related**: cause, composition-division, valid, sound, hidden-premise, ontological (for a priori), teleological, god.
