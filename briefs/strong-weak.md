## Proposed scope (350–500 words)
1. **Definition** (Bland p. 19): an inductive argument is strong when its premises, if true, make the conclusion probable; weak when they do not. Strength comes in degrees ("shades of grey").
2. **Strength is not truth**: strength is about support; whether the premises are true is a separate question. Strong + true premises = *cogent* in some texts (same wording as `sound`). A strong argument with true premises can still have a false conclusion (Bland pp. 91–92, Santa or Titanic; one only).
3. **What affects strength**: amount and quality of evidence; relevance; how strongly the conclusion is stated (a more cautious conclusion is easier to support, Bland p. 99); new evidence can strengthen or weaken an argument, unlike validity. Point to type-specific criteria in `generalisation` and `analogy`.
4. **Worked comparison**: one conclusion supported by a weak, then a stronger, version of the same argument.
5. **Weak induction and fallacies**: one line and links: hasty generalisation, faulty analogy, gambler's fallacy, post hoc, slippery slope, ad verecundiam.
6. **Common mistakes**: never "valid", "sound", "true" for an inductive argument; never "proves" (style guide §2).
7. **Related**: induction, deductive vs inductive, valid, sound, generalisation, analogy, the weak-induction fallacies.
