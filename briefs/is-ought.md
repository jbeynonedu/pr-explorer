## Proposed scope (450–700 (see Q1) words)
1. **Definition** (Bland p. 14; p. 68): drawing a normative conclusion (what ought to be) from purely descriptive premises (what is), with no normative premise to bridge the gap.
2. **Primary text**: David Hume (1711–1776), *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), Book 3, Part 1, §1, the "is ... ought" passage. Public domain; quoted from a fetched edition, with a plain-English gloss.
3. **The pattern**: `1. [Descriptive premise(s) only].` / `C. [Normative conclusion].`
4. **Examples** (2): fresh ones, not the cigarette example (already used in descriptive and normative claims and hidden premise). Avoid taking a side on a live issue.
5. **Repairing it, and when it is not fallacious**: often the normative premise is hidden; once supplied, the argument may be valid, and the debate moves to whether that premise is true. Link hidden premise; do not repeat it.
6. **A contested principle** (see Q2): how Hume's passage should be read is disputed, and some philosophers deny that an *ought* can never follow from an *is* (e.g. John Searle's 1964 promising argument). Presented even-handedly, no verdict.
7. **Distinguish**: illicit appeal to nature; G. E. Moore's "naturalistic fallacy" (see section Q5).
8. **Related**: descriptive and normative claims, hidden premise, illicit appeal to nature, valid, morality, value.
