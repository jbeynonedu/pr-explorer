## Proposed scope (300–500 words)
1. **Definition**: from two conditionals where the consequent of the first is the antecedent of the second, conclude a conditional linking the first antecedent to the last consequent; the conclusion is itself a conditional (Bland pp. 27–28). Also called a chain argument (Bland: "conditional chain").
2. **The form**: `P ⊃ Q, Q ⊃ R ∴ P ⊃ R`, standard form beside symbolic form.
3. **Example**: fresh Australian example with dictionary.
4. **Why it is valid**: eight-row truth table; the conclusion is false in only two rows, and each has a false premise (Bland p. 52). Interactive (needs the widget extended to three letters; see note).
5. **Variations**: longer chains with any number of links (Bland p. 28); premises given in the other order; negated parts.
6. **Look-alike forms**, each with its counterexample row checked by code: links that do not join, e.g. `P ⊃ Q, R ⊃ Q ∴ P ⊃ R` and `P ⊃ Q, P ⊃ R ∴ Q ⊃ R` (invalid); reversed conclusion `P ⊃ Q, Q ⊃ R ∴ R ⊃ P` (invalid).
7. **Where it matters**: [slippery slope](slippery-slope) arguments are often chains of conditionals. The chain's form is valid; whether the argument is any good depends on whether every link is true (one line; link only).
