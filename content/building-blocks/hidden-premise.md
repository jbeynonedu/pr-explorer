# Hidden premise

**A hidden premise is an assumption that an argument needs in order to work but does not state.** It is also called a *tacit premise*, an *assumed premise* or simply an *assumption*. In [standard form](standard-form), a hidden premise is written in and labelled (HP).

## Why premises go unstated

A premise is often left out because it seems obvious or is widely shared. Sometimes, though, it is the controversial part of the argument; writing it in lets you evaluate it.

## Example

"Those biscuits must be good because Jessica baked them."

```sf
1. Jessica baked the biscuits.
C. Therefore, the biscuits are good.
```

The argument relies on something unstated about Jessica:

```sf
1. Jessica baked the biscuits.
2. Jessica is skilled at baking biscuits. (HP)
C. Therefore, the biscuits are good.
```

## Choosing the hidden premise

Ask what else must be true for the stated premises to support the conclusion. There is often more than one candidate:

- With "Everything Jessica bakes is good", the argument becomes deductively [valid](valid), but the premise is hard to believe.
- With "Jessica is skilled at baking biscuits", the premise is more plausible, but the argument is inductive: it makes the conclusion probable, not certain.

The *principle of charity* says to reconstruct an argument in its best form: supply the most plausible premise that makes it valid (if deductive) or inductively strong (if inductive).

A hidden premise is often needed where a [normative](descriptive-normative) conclusion is drawn from descriptive premises. "Cigarette smoking causes ill health, so the government should ban it" needs a normative premise, such as "The government has a duty to protect public health" (see [is-ought fallacy](is-ought)). That premise bridges the gap from *is* to *ought*; a valid version would need more, such as a premise that a ban is the way to meet that duty.

## A common mistake

Do not simply add "If [the stated premises], then [the conclusion]." A premise of that form makes any argument [valid](valid), so it hides the real assumption instead of identifying it. Look for the specific claim the argument relies on, and add the conditional only if you can say why it is true.

## Hidden conclusions

Sometimes the premises are stated and the conclusion is only implied. Write the conclusion in and label it (HC):

```sf
1. Eating junk food every day leads to health problems like obesity and diabetes.
2. Junk food is high in fat, sugar, and salt.
C. Therefore, you should avoid eating junk food every day. (HC)
```

If the implied conclusion is vague, state the one the arguer most plausibly intends.
