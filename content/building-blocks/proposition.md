# Proposition

**A proposition is a declarative statement that is capable of being true or false.** Propositions are the building blocks of [arguments](argument): every [premise](premise) and every [conclusion](conclusion) is a proposition.

## Truth value

Being true or being false is a proposition's *truth value*. Classical logic, the logic used in this course, follows two principles: *bivalence* (each proposition has exactly one truth value) and *non-contradiction* (no proposition is both true and false at once). Some modern systems, such as fuzzy logic, allow degrees of truth between true and false, but the syllabus does not use them.

A proposition only has to be *capable* of truth or falsity. We may not know its truth value, and its truth value may depend on when, where or by whom it is asserted. Bland's examples:

- "Today is Tuesday." True on some days and false on others.
- "The Prime Minister of our country is Anthony Albanese." True or false depending on which country "our" refers to.
- "Capital punishment should be abolished." People disagree about whether it is true. Bland treats normative statements like this as propositions. Note, though, that some moral philosophers (non-cognitivists) deny that moral statements are true or false in any substantial sense; see [descriptive and normative claims](descriptive-normative).

## What is not a proposition

Sentences that cannot be true or false are not propositions:

- questions: "Where is my phone?"
- commands: "Please sit down."
- exclamations: "Wow, that's amazing!"

## Sentences and propositions

A proposition is what a declarative sentence says, not the words used to say it. Different sentences can express the same proposition. "Snow is white" and the German "Schnee ist weiss" use different words but say the same thing. Logic evaluates the proposition, so when you put an argument into [standard form](standard-form), check whether two differently worded premises really express the same proposition.

## Proposition and claim

The syllabus lists both terms. In this course they are near-synonyms: a [claim](claim) is a proposition that someone puts forward as true.

## Propositions in symbolic logic

Simple propositions are combined into compound ones with [logical operators](logical-operators). Each simple proposition gets a letter in a *dictionary* (`L = "I win the lotto."` `W = "I am wealthy."`), so "If I win the lotto, then I am wealthy" becomes `L ⊃ W`. See [translating and symbolising propositions](translating-symbolising).
