# Descriptive and normative claims

**A descriptive claim says how things are. A normative claim says how things ought to be, or makes a judgement of value.** The distinction is not a syllabus term, but you need it to spot the [is-ought fallacy](is-ought) and many [hidden premises](hidden-premise).

## The two kinds

| Descriptive (factual) | Normative (evaluative) |
| --- | --- |
| Concerned with what *is* | Concerned with what *ought to be* |
| "Tasmania is south of Victoria." | "You should not steal." |
| "Cats have four legs." | "Lying is wrong." |

Normative claims include judgements about things, people, relationships and actions: what is right or wrong, good or bad, better or worse.

A descriptive claim can be false. "Cats have six legs" is still descriptive; it is just untrue.

## Telling them apart

Words such as *should*, *ought*, *must*, *wrong*, *good* and *better* often mark a normative claim. They are a guide only. "It must be raining" is a descriptive claim about the weather, and "Your answer to question 3 is wrong" usually means only that it is incorrect.

## Why it matters in arguments

There is a logical gap between descriptive premises and a normative conclusion. Consider:

```sf
1. Cigarette smoking causes ill health.
C. Therefore, the government should ban cigarette smoking.
```

The premise says what is; the conclusion says what the government ought to do. The argument needs a normative premise, such as "The government has a duty to protect public health." See [hidden premise](hidden-premise) and the [is-ought fallacy](is-ought).

## Can normative claims be true?

This entry, like the [proposition](proposition) entry, treats normative claims as true or false. Philosophers disagree about this. *Cognitivists* hold that moral claims express beliefs and can be true or false. *Non-cognitivists* deny that moral claims are true or false in any substantial sense.
