# Purposes of argument (explain, justify, analyse, evaluate)

**The syllabus names four purposes of argument: to explain, to justify, to analyse and to evaluate.** Justifying and explaining are things an [argument](argument) does. Analysing and evaluating are things you do *to* an argument. The definitions below adapt the QCAA's descriptions of these verbs.

## Justify

To justify a [claim](claim) is to give reasons that show it is right or reasonable to accept. This is the central job of most arguments: the conclusion is in question, and the premises are offered to settle it.

## Explain

To explain is to make something clear by revealing a cause, reason, context or relationship. An explanation can have the same premise-and-conclusion structure as a justification, but a different purpose. In a justification, the conclusion is in doubt and the premises give reasons to believe it. In an explanation, the conclusion is already accepted and the premises say *why* it is so.

| | Example | What is in doubt? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explanation | "The road is wet because it rained overnight." | Not that the road is wet, but why. |
| Justification | "It must have rained overnight, because the road is wet." | Whether it rained. |

Both use "because", so indicator words will not tell them apart. Ask whether the conclusion is being established or accounted for. (Some logicians treat explanations as a separate kind of thing from arguments; here, following the syllabus, an explanation counts as an argument with a different purpose.)

## Analyse

To analyse an argument is to break it down to reveal aspects that are not immediately obvious: identify its premises and conclusion, set it out in [standard form](standard-form), and find any [hidden premises](hidden-premise).

## Evaluate

To evaluate an argument is to make a judgement about its quality, using appropriate criteria: whether the premises are true, and whether they support the conclusion ([validity](valid) and [soundness](sound) for deductive arguments, [strength](strong-weak) for inductive ones).

Justifying is also different from persuading: an argument can persuade without justifying, and the reverse. See [justification and persuasion](justification-persuasion).
