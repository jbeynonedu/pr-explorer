# Claim

**A claim is a statement that asserts something to be true or false.** "Claim" and "[proposition](proposition)" are close to synonyms. The difference is one of emphasis: a claim is a proposition that someone puts forward as true.

## Claims and arguments

A claim on its own gives no reason to accept it. "Humans lack free will" is a claim. It can be a good starting point for discussion, but stated by itself it is not an [argument](argument). An argument supports a claim with reasons, its [premises](premise).

## Claims inside arguments

Premises and [conclusions](conclusion) are normally claims: propositions put forward as true. The premises of an argument can therefore be challenged like any other claim:

```sf
1. Mobile phones distract students in class.
2. Schools should ban things that distract students in class.
C. Therefore, schools should ban mobile phones in class.
```

Both premises are claims, and an evaluation can ask what supports each one. That support (a study, an example, a further argument) does not have to appear in the standard form, but it should exist somewhere.

## Evaluating claims

Claims are [true or false](true-false). They can also be assessed as more or less [plausible](plausible) and [credible](credible). Arguments are not true or false; they are judged by whether their premises are true and whether those premises support the conclusion.

## Claims and essays

The central thesis of an analytical essay is a claim. The essay is the argument that supports it.
