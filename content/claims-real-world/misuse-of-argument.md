# Use and misuse of argument

**Argument is *used* well when reasons are offered that genuinely support a claim. It is *misused* when something that looks like argument is used to persuade without justifying, or when an opponent's argument is misrepresented.** Political discourse, advertising and scientific debate are three places where both happen.

<!-- tab: Overview -->
## Use and misuse

The difference is the one between [justification and persuasion](justification-persuasion). A good argument gives reasons an audience can check. A misused argument borrows the look of reasoning, such as premises, evidence and a conclusion, but the reasons do not support the conclusion, or are not the real reasons at all.

Misuse is not the same as disagreement. An argument for a conclusion you reject can still be a good argument, and an argument for a conclusion you accept can be a bad one. Judge the reasoning, not whether you like where it ends up.

## A toolkit for real-world arguments

1. **Find the claim.** What is the speaker or advertiser trying to get you to accept or do?
2. **Set out the argument** in [standard form](standard-form), adding any [hidden premises](hidden-premise).
3. **Check the premises.** Are they [true](true-false), or at least [plausible](plausible)? Is the source [credible](credible)?
4. **Check the support.** Is a deductive argument [valid](valid)? Is an inductive one [strong](strong-weak)?
5. **Name any fallacy**, and say exactly where the reasoning goes wrong.
6. **Check yourself** for [confirmation bias](confirmation-bias): would you judge the argument the same way if it reached the opposite conclusion?

## Legitimate use

Political debate is often argument at its most important: people offer reasons for decisions that affect everyone. Suppose a council proposes turning a car park into a public plaza. A supporter might argue that foot traffic will rise and that local businesses will benefit; an opponent might argue that shoppers who drive will go elsewhere. Both are real arguments. Their premises can be tested, for example with counts of how shoppers arrive.

## Common misuses

The same moves turn up on every side. In each pair below, both speakers make the same mistake.

**[Straw man](straw-man):** distorting the other side's position so it is easier to attack.

- Supporter: "The opponents want the town centre to stay a car park forever."
- Opponent: "The supporters want to drive every car out of town."

**[Ad hominem](ad-hominem):** attacking the person instead of the argument.

- Supporter: "The objectors are just shop owners protecting their profits."
- Opponent: "The councillor behind this owns a café on the square."

An interest in the outcome is a reason to check a person's claims more carefully. It does not show that their argument is wrong.

**Red herring:** changing the subject. Asked how many parking spaces will be lost, a speaker talks about how attractive the new plaza will look. The question is never answered.

## The remedy: charity

The principle of charity asks you to interpret an opponent's argument in its strongest, most reasonable form before evaluating it. Straw men, personal attacks and red herrings all avoid the strongest version of the other side's case.

## The implied argument

Most advertisements contain an argument with an unstated conclusion: *you should buy this*. Consider this made-up skincare slogan:

> "Dermatologist-developed formula. Loved by over a million Australians."

```sf
1. The product was developed by a dermatologist.
2. Over a million Australians like the product.
(HP) A product that was developed by an expert and is popular is effective.
C. Therefore, you should buy it.
```

The first premise appeals to relevant expertise, which is reasonable as far as it goes, but it does not show that the product works (see [ad verecundiam](ad-verecundiam)). The second is an appeal to popularity ([ad populum](ad-populum)). The hidden premise is doing most of the work, and it is doubtful.

Other common techniques have their own entries: the [illicit appeal to nature](appeal-to-nature) and appeals to fear or other emotions ([ad misericordiam](ad-misericordiam)).

## What the law says

In Australia, the Australian Consumer Law states:

> A person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive.
>
> — *Competition and Consumer Act 2010* (Cth), Schedule 2, s 18(1)

According to the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC), it does not matter whether a business intended to mislead, and leaving out important information can itself be misleading. In 2022, for example, the Federal Court ordered Samsung to pay $14 million in penalties over advertisements showing phones being used in pools and the sea, when pool and sea water could damage them.

The legal standard is not the logical standard. Wildly exaggerated claims that no one would take seriously ("puffery", such as "the best steaks on earth") are generally not considered misleading in law. They are still not reasons to buy.

## Legitimate disagreement

Scientists disagree, and disagreement is part of how science works: rival hypotheses are tested, and new evidence can overturn accepted views. Scientific conclusions are reached by induction, so even well-supported ones are [strongly supported](strong-weak), not proved with certainty (see [Hume's problem of induction](problem-of-induction)).

## Common misuses

- **Demanding proof that induction cannot give.** Because no inductive conclusion is certain, it is always possible to say "that hasn't been proven". The real question is how strong the evidence is.
- **Overstating a single result.** One study reported as "scientists prove X" claims more certainty than one study can provide.
- **Cherry-picking.** Citing only the studies that support a view and ignoring those that do not; see [confirmation bias](confirmation-bias).
- **The wrong authority.** Relying on someone who has no expertise in the relevant field; see [ad verecundiam](ad-verecundiam).

## Manufacturing doubt

Doubt can also be pursued as a goal in itself. A 1969 internal document from the tobacco company Brown & Williamson, titled "Smoking and Health Proposal", stated:

> Doubt is our product since it is the best means of competing with the 'body of fact' that exists in the mind of the general public. It is also the means of establishing a controversy.
>
> — "Smoking and Health Proposal" (Brown & Williamson, 1969), UCSF Industry Documents Library

The aim described here is not to weigh the evidence but to create the appearance of a controversy. It turns a genuine feature of science, that its conclusions are open to revision, into a tool for avoiding them.
