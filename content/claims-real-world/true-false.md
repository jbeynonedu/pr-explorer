# True and false

**A claim is true if things are as it says they are, and false if they are not.** True and false are the two *truth values*. [Propositions](proposition) and [claims](claim) have truth values; [arguments](argument) do not.

## What makes a claim true

The working account in this Explorer is the *correspondence theory*: a claim is true if it corresponds to the facts. "Brisbane is the capital of Queensland" is true because Brisbane is the capital of Queensland. In one survey's words, what we believe or say is true "if it corresponds to the way things actually are – to the facts" (Glanzberg, "Truth", §1.1.1).

Philosophers dispute what truth is, and there are rival accounts:

- **Coherence theory**: a belief is true if it belongs to a coherent system of beliefs. Coherence here is stronger than mere consistency.
- **Pragmatist theories**: truth is what would remain settled at the end of inquiry, or what it is satisfactory to believe. True beliefs will not conflict with later experience.

These questions belong to metaphysics and epistemology.

## Truth is not belief, certainty or proof

- On the correspondence account, a claim can be true when no one believes it, and false when everyone does.
- You can be uncertain whether a claim is true. It still has a truth value; you just do not know which. Because certainty is rare in real-world arguments, evaluation often relies on whether a claim is [plausible](plausible) and on the weight of evidence (see [sound](sound)).
- The premises of a [strong](strong-weak) inductive argument, if true, make its conclusion probable. They do not guarantee that it is true: inductive conclusions are supported, not proved.
- "Not proven" does not mean "false".

## How claims are checked

*Empirical* claims depend on facts about the world, so they are checked against evidence: "The library opens at 8 am" is settled by finding out when it opens. Other claims are commonly said to be true because of what their words mean, such as "All bachelors are unmarried". Whether [normative](descriptive-normative) claims, claims about what should be, are true or false in the same way is itself disputed.

## Truth and argument quality

Truth belongs to the premises and conclusion, not to the argument. A [valid](valid) argument can have a false conclusion if a premise is false:

```sf
1. All birds can fly.
2. Penguins are birds.
C. Therefore, penguins can fly.
```

The form is valid, but premise 1 is false, so the argument is unsound. An argument is [sound](sound) only when it is valid *and* all its premises are true.

## Common mistakes

- **"This argument is true."** Say instead that it is valid or invalid, sound or unsound, or strong or weak.
- **"It's true for me."** Ask what is meant. It may mean "I believe it", or that the claim is true *of* the speaker ("Spicy food upsets my stomach"). It may also express *relativism about truth*. This is the philosophical view that what is true for one person or group may not be true for another, with no neutral standpoint to decide between them. That view is debated in philosophy; the phrase alone does not establish it.
