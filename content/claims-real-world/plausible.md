# Plausible

**A claim is plausible if it makes sense on face value: nothing about the claim itself makes it hard to believe.** This is more than being merely possible, and less than being [credible](credible) or [true](true-false). Plausibility is judged *prima facie*, on first examination, from the claim itself.

"Less" here means a weaker standard, not a step on a ladder: a claim can be credible, or even true, without seeming plausible at first.

## Possible, plausible, credible, true

These four ideas are easily confused:

- **Possible**: the claim could be true. "I will win the lottery every week this year" is possible, but it is not plausible.
- **Plausible**: the claim makes sense given what you already know. "Most students at this school live within 20 km of it" is plausible; "Students at this school sleep fourteen hours a night" is not.
- **Credible**: the claim is worth believing given its support, especially the reliability of its source. Plausibility ignores who said it and what evidence is offered for it; credibility does not.
- **True**: things are as the claim says they are.

## Degrees and background knowledge

Plausibility comes in degrees, and it depends on background knowledge. A claim that seems plausible to one person may seem implausible to someone who knows more, and plausibility can change as knowledge grows.

So plausibility is not a test of truth:

- **Plausible claims can lack support.** Claims such as "eating carrots improves your eyesight" or "cracking your knuckles causes arthritis" sound reasonable and are widely believed, but have limited evidence behind them.
- **Implausible claims can be true.** "A sheet of paper 0.1 mm thick, if it could be folded in half 42 times, would be more than 400,000 km thick" sounds absurd. Each fold doubles the thickness, though, and 0.1 mm doubled 42 times is about 440,000 km.

## Plausibility in evaluating arguments

When a premise is contested and cannot be settled by checking a simple fact, a good first question is whether it is at least plausible. An implausible premise cannot be accepted as true when judging soundness. A plausible one still needs the weight of evidence behind it before you accept it; see [sound](sound) for this working standard.

```sf
1. Students who sleep too little tend to concentrate less well in class.
2. Most students at this school sleep less than four hours a night.
C. Therefore, most students at this school probably concentrate less well in class.
```

Premise 1 is plausible: it fits what most people know about tiredness, though it still needs evidence. Premise 2 is implausible: it conflicts with what most people know about how much sleep people need, so it should not be accepted without strong evidence.

Whether premises are plausible or well supported is a question about their acceptability. It is separate from the argument's [strength](strong-weak), which concerns how well the premises support the conclusion if they are true.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "plausible" as "credible".** A claim from an unreliable source can be plausible; a surprising claim from a reliable source can be credible without seeming plausible at first.
- **Calling an argument plausible when you mean strong.** Plausibility belongs to claims. Arguments are [valid or invalid](valid), [sound or unsound](sound), or [strong or weak](strong-weak).
