# Analogy

**An analogy compares two things, drawing attention to ways in which they are alike. An argument from analogy uses such a comparison to conclude that the two things are probably alike in a further respect.** The argument from analogy is one of the two most important kinds of [inductive](induction) argument; the other is [generalisation](generalisation).

## Analogies that explain and analogies that argue

Not every analogy is an argument from analogy. Many analogies explain: they help you understand something unfamiliar by comparing it with something familiar. "The heart is a pump" helps explain what the heart does; it is not offered as evidence that the heart has some further property. An explanatory analogy explains rather than justifies, and is judged by how well it clarifies (see [purposes of argument](purposes-of-argument)).

An argument from analogy goes a step further. It uses the comparison as evidence that one of the two things has a property not yet observed in it.

## The pattern

```sf
1. A and B are alike in respects r1, r2, r3...
2. A has property Q.
C. Therefore, B probably has property Q.
```

The conclusion is probable, not guaranteed, so the argument is judged as [strong or weak](strong-weak).

## Judging its strength

Five criteria help to judge an argument from analogy:

1. **Truth**: are A and B really alike in the ways claimed?
2. **Relevance**: do the shared respects bear on property Q? Similarities that have nothing to do with Q add no support.
3. **Number**: other things being equal, more relevant similarities give a stronger argument.
4. **Diversity**: the more diverse the kinds of similarity, the stronger the argument.
5. **Disanalogy**: are there differences between A and B that bear on Q? A single relevant difference can outweigh many similarities.

Relevance matters most, for similarities and differences alike. The criteria can also point different ways in the same case, so applying them takes judgement. When the similarities are not relevant enough to support the conclusion, or relevant differences are ignored, the argument commits the fallacy of [faulty analogy](faulty-analogy).

## Worked example

A coach argues: "The new training program worked for our Under 15 netball team, so it will probably work for our Under 17s."

```sf
1. The Under 15 and Under 17 teams train at the same club, with the same coaches, for the same hours each week, and play in similar competitions.
2. The new training program improved the Under 15 team's fitness and results.
C. Therefore, the new program will probably improve the Under 17 team's fitness and results.
```

| Criterion | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Truth | Plausible, if the teams really do share coaches, hours and competitions. |
| Relevance | Coaching, training time and level of competition all bear on how a program works. Strengthens. |
| Number | Several relevant similarities. Strengthens. |
| Diversity | The similarities are of different kinds (people, time, competition). Strengthens. |
| Disanalogy | The players are older and at a different stage of development, so the program may suit one age group better. Weakens somewhat. |

Overall, a moderately strong argument. It would be stronger with evidence that the program has also worked for older players.

## In science

Analogies are used in science both to suggest new ideas and to support them. In 1934, a pharmacologist named Schaumann was testing compounds, chemically similar to morphine, for a quite different purpose: relieving spasms. One compound, meperidine, caused a reaction in mice that had only ever been seen with morphine. Reasoning by analogy, he suggested it might also act like morphine as a narcotic. Later tests, first on animals and finally on people, confirmed that it relieves pain. The analogy suggested the hypothesis; testing then supported it. Testing a drug on animals before people relies on analogy too.

## A famous argument from analogy

The watchmaker argument of William Paley (1743–1805) argues for a creator God from similarities between human-made designs, such as a watch, and the natural world (see the [teleological argument](teleological-argument)). Whether its analogy is strong or faulty is debated; it is judged by the same criteria.
