# Analogy

**An analogy is a comparison between two things that highlights respects in which they are similar. An argument from analogy uses such a comparison to conclude that, because the two things are alike in some respects, they are probably alike in a further respect.** It is one of the two main kinds of [inductive](induction) argument; the other is [generalisation](generalisation).

## Analogies that explain and analogies that argue

Not every analogy is an argument. Many analogies explain: they help you understand something unfamiliar by comparing it with something familiar. "The heart is a pump" explains what the heart does; it offers no evidence for a conclusion. An explanatory analogy is judged by how well it clarifies (see [purposes of argument](purposes-of-argument)).

An argument from analogy goes a step further. It uses the comparison as evidence that one of the two things has a property not yet observed in it.

## The pattern

```sf
1. A and B are alike in respects r1, r2, r3...
2. A has property Q.
C. Therefore, B probably has property Q.
```

The conclusion is probable, not guaranteed, so the argument is judged as [strong or weak](strong-weak).

## How strong is it?

Five criteria help to judge an argument from analogy:

1. **Truth**: are A and B really alike in the ways claimed?
2. **Relevance**: do the shared respects bear on property Q? Similarities that have nothing to do with Q add no support.
3. **Number**: other things being equal, more relevant similarities give a stronger argument.
4. **Diversity**: similarities of several different kinds give a stronger argument than many similarities of one kind.
5. **Disanalogy**: are there differences between A and B that bear on Q? A single relevant difference can outweigh many similarities.

Relevance usually decides the matter, for similarities and differences alike. The criteria can also pull in different directions, so applying them takes judgement. When the similarities are not relevant enough to support the conclusion, or relevant differences are ignored, the argument commits the fallacy of [faulty analogy](faulty-analogy).

## Worked example

A coach argues: "The new training program worked for our Under 15 netball team, so it will probably work for our Under 17s."

```sf
1. The Under 15 and Under 17 teams train at the same club, with the same coaches, for the same hours each week, and play in similar competitions.
2. The new training program improved the Under 15 team's fitness and results.
C. Therefore, the new program will probably improve the Under 17 team's fitness and results.
```

| Criterion | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Truth | Plausible, if the teams really do share coaches, hours and competitions. |
| Relevance | Coaching, training time and level of competition all bear on how a program works. Strengthens. |
| Number | Several relevant similarities. Strengthens. |
| Diversity | The similarities are of different kinds (people, time, competition). Strengthens. |
| Disanalogy | The players are older and at a different stage of development, and the program may suit one age group better. Weakens somewhat. |

Overall, a moderately strong argument. It would be stronger with evidence that the program has also worked for older players.

## In science

Analogies are used in science both to suggest new ideas and to support them. In 1934, a pharmacologist named Schaumann was testing synthetic compounds with a chemical structure similar to morphine. One of them, meperidine, produced an effect in mice that had previously been seen only with morphine. By analogy, he conjectured that it might also share morphine's narcotic effects. Testing on rats, rabbits, dogs and eventually humans showed that meperidine, like morphine, was an effective painkiller. Testing a drug on animals before humans relies on the same kind of reasoning.

## A famous argument from analogy

The watchmaker argument of William Paley (1743–1805) compares living things with a watch to argue for a designer. It is a version of the [teleological argument](teleological-argument), and whether its analogy is strong or faulty is debated; it is judged by the same criteria.
