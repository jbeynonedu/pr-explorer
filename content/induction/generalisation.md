# Generalisation

**An argument by generalisation draws a conclusion about a whole group from observations of a sample of that group.** It is one of the two main kinds of [inductive](induction) argument; the other is [analogy](analogy).

## The pattern

```sf
1. A sample of members of group A have property B.
C. Therefore, probably all (or most) members of group A have property B.
```

The sample details and the observed property can be given in one premise or split across two.

## All, most, or a proportion

A generalisation may be about *all* members of a group ("all As are B"), or about *most* of them or a stated proportion ("most As are B", "about 70% of As are B"). The difference matters when a counterexample turns up. A single A that is not B shows that "all As are B" is false. It does not show that "most As are B" is false.

## Judging its strength

Four criteria help to judge the [strength](strong-weak) of an argument by generalisation:

1. **Sample size**: is the sample large enough? Other things being equal, a larger sample gives a stronger argument. How large it needs to be depends on how varied the group is: one taste of a well-stirred soup can tell you about the whole pot.
2. **Representativeness**: is the sample a fair cross-section of the whole group, reflecting its variety? The more representative, the stronger.
3. **Bias**: is the sample free from one-sided selection? A biased sample weakens the argument.
4. **Conclusion scope**: how is the conclusion qualified? A cautious conclusion ("most", "probably") is easier to support than a sweeping one ("all").

## Worked example

```sf
1. A survey of 1,500 Queensland Year 12 students, drawn from state, Catholic and independent schools in every region in proportion to their enrolments, found that 81% had a part-time job or regular volunteer role.
C. Therefore, most Queensland Year 12 students probably have a part-time job or regular volunteer role.
```

*This survey is invented for illustration.*

- **Sample size**: 1,500 is large. This strengthens the argument.
- **Representativeness**: every school sector and region is included, in proportion to its size. This strengthens it.
- **Bias**: nothing suggests one-sided selection, though we would want to know how students were chosen and how many declined. On the information given, this does not weaken it.
- **Conclusion scope**: "most" and "probably" are cautious. This strengthens it.

Overall, this is a strong argument. It would become a weak one if, say, the sample were twelve students from one school. A conclusion of "all" would fare worse still: the survey itself rules it out, since 19% of the sample had neither. A generalisation whose evidence is clearly inadequate commits the fallacy of [hasty generalisation](hasty-generalisation).

## In science

Much scientific reasoning generalises from samples. A medical trial tests a treatment on a sample of patients and draws a conclusion about patients in general. A result can mislead if the sample happens to be unusual, which is one reason trials are repeated in different circumstances. Opinion polls also generalise from a sample, and use statistics to state how confident the estimate is. A claim about a general law of nature goes beyond any sample, however large, which is where [Hume's problem of induction](problem-of-induction) arises; see also the [hypothetico-deductive method](hypothetico-deductive).

## A generalisation as a premise

An argument that merely *uses* a general claim as a premise is not an argument by generalisation. The generalising happens only when the argument itself moves from a sample to the group. See [hasty generalisation](hasty-generalisation).
