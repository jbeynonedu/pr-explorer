# Generalisation

**An argument by generalisation draws a conclusion about a whole group from observations of a sample of that group.** It is one of the two main kinds of [inductive](induction) argument; the other is [analogy](analogy).

## The pattern

```sf
1. A sample of members of group A have property B.
C. Therefore, probably all (or most) members of group A have property B.
```

The sample details and the observed property can be given in one premise or split across two.

## Universal and statistical generalisations

A generalisation may be **universal** ("all As are B") or **statistical** ("most As are B", "about 70% of As are B"). The difference matters when a counterexample turns up. A single A that is not B shows that a universal generalisation is false. It does not show that a statistical one is false.

## How strong is it?

Four criteria help to judge the [strength](strong-weak) of an argument by generalisation:

1. **Sample size**: is the sample large enough? Other things being equal, a larger sample gives a stronger argument.
2. **Representativeness**: is the sample drawn fairly from the whole group, so that it reflects the group's variety? The more representative, the stronger.
3. **Bias**: is the sample free from one-sided selection? A biased sample weakens the argument.
4. **Conclusion scope**: how is the conclusion qualified? A cautious conclusion ("most", "probably") is easier to support than a sweeping one ("all").

## Worked example

```sf
1. A survey of 1,500 Queensland Year 12 students, drawn from state, Catholic and independent schools in every region, found that 81% had a part-time job or regular volunteer role.
C. Therefore, most Queensland Year 12 students probably have a part-time job or regular volunteer role.
```

*This survey is invented for illustration.*

- **Sample size**: 1,500 is large. This strengthens the argument.
- **Representativeness**: every school sector and region is included. This strengthens it.
- **Bias**: nothing suggests one-sided selection, though we would want to know how students were chosen and how many declined. On the information given, this does not weaken it.
- **Conclusion scope**: "most" and "probably" are cautious. This strengthens it.

Overall, this is a strong argument. It would become a weak one if, say, the sample were twelve students from one school, or the conclusion said "all". A generalisation whose evidence is clearly inadequate commits the fallacy of [hasty generalisation](hasty-generalisation).

## In science

Much scientific reasoning generalises from samples. A medical trial tests a treatment on a sample of patients and draws a conclusion about patients in general. A result can mislead if the sample happens to be unusual, which is one reason trials are repeated in different circumstances. Opinion polls also generalise from a sample, and use statistics to state how confident the estimate is.

## A generalisation as a premise

An argument that merely *uses* a general claim as a premise is not an argument by generalisation. The generalising happens only when the argument itself moves from a sample to the group. See [hasty generalisation](hasty-generalisation).
