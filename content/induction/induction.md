# Induction

**An inductive argument is an argument that aims to make its conclusion probable, not to guarantee it.** Its premises are meant to support the conclusion without making it certain. Induction is reasoning of this kind.

## What makes an argument inductive

What unites inductive arguments is the kind of link between premises and conclusion: the premises support the conclusion without guaranteeing it. Even if every premise is true, the conclusion could still be false. Inductive arguments are the most common kind in everyday life, and much of science relies on them.

Definitions vary. On the broad definition used here, any argument that aims at probable rather than certain support is inductive. Some texts use a narrower definition, restricted to recognised patterns such as generalisation, analogy and causal inference.

## Example

```sf
1. Six people at the barbecue ate the chicken salad, and all six became ill within hours.
2. Nobody who skipped the chicken salad became ill.
C. Therefore, the chicken salad probably caused the illness.
```

This argument reasons from a pattern of cases to a conclusion about a cause. The premises make the conclusion likely, but do not guarantee it: something else eaten only by those six, or a coincidence, could explain the pattern.

## Kinds of inductive argument

Inductive arguments take many forms. The two most important are generalisation and analogy. Other common kinds include causal reasoning and prediction from past cases.

- **[Generalisation](generalisation)**: from a sample to a conclusion about the whole group it comes from.
- **[Analogy](analogy)**: from the similarity of two things to the conclusion that they are probably alike in a further respect.
- **Causal reasoning**: from evidence such as sequence or correlation to a conclusion about [cause](cause), as in the example above (see [post hoc ergo propter hoc](post-hoc)).
- **Prediction from past cases**: from what has happened so far to what will happen next.

## How inductive arguments are judged

An inductive argument is [strong or weak](strong-weak), not valid or invalid. Strength comes in degrees: one inductive argument can be stronger than another. See [the distinction between deductive and inductive arguments](deductive-vs-inductive).

## Limitations of induction

**Inductive conclusions are fallible.** An argument can seem strong on all the evidence available, with true premises, and still have a false conclusion. Suppose someone in Europe in the 1600s reasoned like this:

```sf
1. Every swan I have observed has been white.
C. Therefore, all swans are white.
```

The swans native to Europe are all white, so the premise could well have been true. But the black swan is native to Australia. In January 1697, the expedition of the Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh recorded black swans on the river now called the Swan River. The river was named after them. The conclusion was false all along. Its weakness was that every swan observed came from one part of the world, and nothing observed in Europe could have revealed this. Even an argument that really is strong, with true premises, can have a false conclusion; see [inductively strong and weak](strong-weak).

This is why inductive conclusions are held open to revision in the light of new evidence. A further limitation is more general. Inductive reasoning from past to future seems to assume that nature will go on behaving as it has. David Hume (1711–1776) is usually read as arguing that this assumption cannot be supported by reasoning without arguing in a circle. See [Hume's problem of induction](problem-of-induction).
