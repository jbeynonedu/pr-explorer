# Induction

**An inductive argument is an argument that aims to make its conclusion probable, not to guarantee it.** Its premises are meant to support the conclusion without making it certain. Induction is reasoning of this kind.

## What makes an argument inductive

What unites inductive arguments is the kind of link between premises and conclusion: the premises support the conclusion without guaranteeing it. Even if every premise is true, the conclusion could still be false. Inductive arguments are the most common kind in everyday life, and much of science relies on them.

Definitions vary. On the broad definition used here, any argument that aims at probable rather than certain support is inductive. Some texts use a narrower definition, restricted to recognised patterns such as generalisation, analogy, causal inference and statistical syllogism.

## Example

```sf
1. The 7:40 bus has arrived late on each of the last twenty school days.
C. Therefore, the 7:40 bus will probably arrive late tomorrow.
```

This argument reasons from past cases to a new case. The premise makes the conclusion likely, but does not guarantee it: tomorrow could be the first day the bus is on time.

## Kinds of inductive argument

- **[Generalisation](generalisation)**: from a sample to a conclusion about the whole group it comes from.
- **[Analogy](analogy)**: from the similarity of two things to the conclusion that they are probably alike in a further respect.
- **Causal reasoning**: from evidence such as sequence or correlation to a conclusion about [cause](cause) (see [post hoc ergo propter hoc](post-hoc)).
- **Prediction from past cases**, as in the example above.

## How inductive arguments are judged

An inductive argument is [strong or weak](strong-weak), not valid or invalid. Strength comes in degrees: one inductive argument can be stronger than another. See [the distinction between deductive and inductive arguments](deductive-vs-inductive).

## Limitations of induction

**Inductive conclusions are fallible.** A strong argument with true premises can still have a false conclusion:

Suppose someone in Europe in the 1600s reasoned like this:

```sf
1. Every swan I have observed has been white.
C. Therefore, all swans are white.
```

The swans native to Europe are all white, so the premise could well have been true. But the black swan is native to Australia. In January 1697 the Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh's expedition recorded black swans on the river now called the Swan River, which was named after them. The conclusion was false all along.

This is why inductive conclusions are held open to revision in the light of new evidence. A further limitation is more general. Inductive reasoning from past to future seems to assume that nature will go on behaving as it has. David Hume (1711–1776) argued that this assumption cannot be justified by reasoning: not deductively, and not inductively without arguing in a circle: see [Hume's problem of induction](problem-of-induction).
