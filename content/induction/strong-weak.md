# Inductively strong and weak

**An inductive argument is strong when its premises, if true, make its conclusion probable. It is weak when they do not.** Strength is the standard for judging [inductive](induction) arguments, in the way that validity is the standard for deductive ones.

## Strength comes in degrees

Validity is all-or-nothing, but strength is not. One inductive argument can be stronger or weaker than another, depending on how well its premises support its conclusion. Calling an argument "strong" or "weak" is an overall verdict on where it sits in that range.

## A weak and a stronger argument

Both arguments below have the same conclusion.

```sf
1. My three closest friends would like school to start later.
C. Therefore, most students at our school would probably like school to start later.
```

```sf
1. In a survey of 400 students chosen at random from every year level at our school, 70% said they would like school to start later.
C. Therefore, most students at our school would probably like school to start later.
```

The first is weak: three friends are few, and they are unlikely to be typical of the whole school. The second is much stronger: the sample is larger, chosen at random, and drawn from every year level. Even so, its conclusion is not guaranteed.

## Strength is not truth

Strength concerns the link between premises and conclusion: *if* the premises are true, how probable do they make the conclusion? Whether the premises *are* true is a separate question. A strong argument with a false premise does not show that its conclusion is probable. A strong argument whose premises are true is called *cogent* in some texts (the inductive counterpart of [sound](sound)).

Even a strong argument with true premises can have a false conclusion. A young child might reason that Santa Claus exists because friends, parents and Christmas films all say so, and the snacks left out for him are always eaten. Every premise is true, and together they give very strong support, yet the conclusion is false. See also the limitations of induction in [induction](induction).

## What affects strength

- **The amount and quality of the evidence**: for example, how large and how representative a sample is.
- **Relevance**: the premises must bear on the conclusion.
- **How strongly the conclusion is stated**: a cautious conclusion ("most", "probably") is easier to support than a sweeping one ("all", "certainly"), so the same evidence makes a stronger argument for it.
- **New evidence**: an inductive argument can be strengthened or weakened by adding premises.

Generalisation and analogy each have their own criteria: see [generalisation](generalisation) and [analogy](analogy).

## Evaluating an inductive argument overall

Strength is one part of evaluating an inductive argument. One framework sets out five criteria for any good argument:

1. **Structure**: the premises do not contradict each other or the conclusion, and none simply assumes the conclusion.
2. **Relevance**: each premise, if true, gives some reason to accept the conclusion.
3. **Acceptability**: each premise is one that a thoughtful, reasonable person could accept.
4. **Sufficiency**: together, the premises are enough to justify the conclusion.
5. **Rebuttal**: the argument answers the strongest objections to it.

Relevance and sufficiency correspond most closely to strength, although sufficiency assumes the reasons are also acceptable. Acceptability is about whether the premises are [plausible](plausible) or well supported, a separate question from strength, as above. Structure concerns how the argument is built. Rebuttal concerns how it deals with counter-evidence, which can itself weaken an argument.

## Weak induction and fallacies

In the grouping used on the [informal fallacies](informal-fallacy) page, four fallacies are fallacies of weak induction: the premises are relevant to the conclusion, but too weak to support it. They are [hasty generalisation](hasty-generalisation), [faulty analogy](faulty-analogy), the [gambler's fallacy](gamblers-fallacy) and [post hoc ergo propter hoc](post-hoc). Not every weak argument commits a fallacy; often the evidence is simply thin, and deciding when it is "too thin" is a judgement call.

## Common mistakes

- Calling an inductive argument "valid", "invalid", "sound" or "true". It is strong or weak; its premises and conclusion are true or false.
- Saying that an inductive argument "proves" its conclusion. Its premises support the conclusion or make it probable.
