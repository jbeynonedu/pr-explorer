# Ad populum (illicit appeal to popular opinion/bandwagon fallacy)

*Illicit appeal to popular opinion*

**The ad populum fallacy treats the popularity of a claim or practice as if it showed that the claim is true or the practice right.** It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) of relevance. The *bandwagon* version urges you to accept something or do something because many people already have.

## The pattern

```sf
1. Many (or most) people believe that P.
C. Therefore, P.
```

The bandwagon version:

```sf
1. Many (or most) people are doing X.
C. Therefore, you should do X.
```

The problem is the gap between popularity and truth. That something is widely believed or done is not, on its own, strong evidence that it is true or that it should be done. For many centuries, slavery was widely defended as morally acceptable; popular support did not make it so. The premise is often exaggerated too: "everyone" rarely means everyone. Watch for phrases such as "everyone knows" and "millions of people can't be wrong".

## Examples

An advertisement says, "It's Australia's favourite family car. Make it yours."

```sf
1. This is the most popular family car in Australia.
C. Therefore, this is the right car for your family.
```

Popularity may suggest the car is generally well regarded. It does not show that it suits a particular family's needs, budget or safety requirements.

A student tells a friend, "Everyone in our year is getting the new phone. You should too."

```sf
1. Most students in our year are getting the new phone.
C. Therefore, you should get the new phone.
```

This is the bandwagon version. Whether the friend should buy the phone depends on reasons such as cost and need, not merely on how many others are buying it.

## When it is not fallacious

Popularity is relevant in some cases.

- **When the conclusion is about popularity itself.** "This is the most-watched program in Queensland" can be supported by viewing figures.
- **When something is right because people follow it.** Which side of the road to drive on is a convention: driving on the left works here because everyone else does it.
- **When the many have judged for themselves.** Wide agreement among people who have each looked at the evidence can give some inductive support, as when many independent reviewers report the same fault in a product. The mistake lies in overrating how much support it gives.

In each case the question is the same: does the popularity give a genuine reason for this particular conclusion, and how much?

Ad populum appeals to popular opinion or common knowledge. When the appeal is to a specified authority instead, the question is whether that authority is relevant and reliable: see [ad verecundiam](ad-verecundiam).
