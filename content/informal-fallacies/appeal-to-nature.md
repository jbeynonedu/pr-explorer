# Illicit appeal to nature

*Argumentum ad naturam*

**The illicit appeal to nature concludes that something is good because it is natural, or bad because it is unnatural, without any further reason.** It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) of relevance, common in advertising and in everyday judgements about what is good or healthy.

## The pattern

```sf
1. X is natural.
C. Therefore, X is good.
```

or

```sf
1. X is unnatural.
C. Therefore, X is bad.
```

Each version relies on a [hidden premise](hidden-premise). Written in:

```sf
1. Whatever is natural is good. (HP)
2. X is natural.
C. Therefore, X is good.
```

Stated openly, the hidden premise is easy to test, and it fails both ways. Earthquakes, pandemics and melanomas are natural, but hardly good. Seatbelts, eyeglasses and anaesthesia are human-made (unnatural in that sense), but hardly bad.

Counterexamples refute the premise only in its universal form. A weaker version, "Natural things are usually better", is a generalisation that needs evidence. Without that evidence, it gives the conclusion little support.

## Examples

A supplement on a pharmacy shelf is labelled, "100% natural, so it's better for you."

```sf
1. This supplement is 100% natural.
C. Therefore, this supplement is better for you.
```

Whether a product is better for you depends on what it actually does, which is a matter for evidence. Being natural does not settle it.

A shopper says, "Artificial sweeteners aren't natural, so they must be bad for you."

```sf
1. Artificial sweeteners are unnatural.
C. Therefore, artificial sweeteners are bad for you.
```

This is the "unnatural, so bad" version. Whether sweeteners are harmful is a question for evidence, not for their origin.

In a class discussion, a student argues, "Humans are naturally competitive, so competition is a good thing."

```sf
1. Humans are naturally competitive.
C. Therefore, competition is good.
```

Even if the premise is true, the conclusion needs a reason why this natural tendency is a good one.

## "Natural" can mean different things

A word with several meanings can be used in one sense in one part of an argument and another sense elsewhere. "Natural" is sometimes used to mean "not made by humans", sometimes "normal or usual", and sometimes "in keeping with what something is". Someone arguing can slide between these meanings without noticing. "Living in cities is unnatural" may mean only that cities are human-made. Then it gives no reason to think city life is bad.

## When it is not fallacious

- **When "natural" stands for a relevant property that has evidence behind it.** If someone must avoid a particular additive, a product without it may really be better for them. The reason is the missing additive, not naturalness as such.
- **When the link between natural and good is argued for.** Whether natural things have value is a genuine philosophical question. An argument that defends a premise connecting naturalness to value is not committing this fallacy. It should be judged on how well it supports that premise.

## Not to be confused with

- The [is-ought fallacy](is-ought): drawing a normative conclusion from purely descriptive premises. An appeal to nature is often one case of it.
- The "naturalistic fallacy" named by G. E. Moore (1873–1958) in *Principia Ethica* (1903). Despite the name, it is what Moore saw as the error of defining "good" in other terms, such as natural properties. It is not the everyday inference from "natural" to "good".
