# Tu quoque (illicit appeal to hypocrisy)

*"You too"*

**The tu quoque fallacy rejects an argument, advice or criticism because the person giving it does not act on it.** It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) and a special kind of circumstantial [ad hominem](ad-hominem). The fallacy is committed by the person who responds, not by the hypocrite. The hypocrite may be inconsistent, but their argument can still be [sound](sound) or strong.

## The pattern

```sf
1. A argues that one should (or should not) do X, or criticises someone for doing X.
2. A's own conduct does not match this (A does, or has done, X, or fails to do it).
C. Therefore, A's argument (or criticism) fails.
```

Premise 2 may be true, and it may be a fair criticism of A. But it does not show that A's reasons are bad. Whether A's reasons support the conclusion depends on those reasons, not on A's behaviour.

A common form turns a criticism back on the critic: "You can't tell me off for that; you've done it too."

## Examples

A parent tells a teenager to put the phone away at dinner. The teenager answers, "You're always on your phone at dinner, so I don't see why I should put mine away."

```sf
1. The parent argues that the teenager should not use a phone at dinner.
2. The parent uses a phone at dinner.
C. Therefore, the parent's argument fails.
```

The parent may need to change too. That does not show the advice was wrong.

A sports captain urges the team to pick up their rubbish after training. A player replies, "You left your empty water bottles on the oval last week. Why should we listen to you?"

```sf
1. The captain argues that players should pick up their rubbish.
2. The captain has left rubbish on the oval.
C. Therefore, the captain's argument fails.
```

The reasons for picking up rubbish, such as keeping the oval clean and safe, are unaffected by the captain's lapse.

Criticised by a classmate for copying homework answers, a student replies, "You copied answers in Year 9 yourself."

```sf
1. The classmate criticises the student for copying homework answers.
2. The classmate copied answers in Year 9.
C. Therefore, the classmate's criticism fails.
```

This deflects the criticism without answering it. Whether copying is wrong does not depend on whether the critic has done it too.

## When it is not fallacious

Pointing out inconsistency can be relevant when the question is not whether the argument is sound or strong.

- **Trust and testimony.** If you are relying only on someone's word, inconsistency between what they say and do can be a reason to look more closely. Hypocrisy is a reason for suspicion, but not enough on its own to dismiss what they say.
- **Fairness and consistency.** "You do it too" can be a fair request that a rule apply to everyone, including the person who set it. Read charitably, the teenager may mean only this. It becomes tu quoque when offered as a reason to think the advice is wrong.
- **Judging the person.** Inconsistency can be relevant to whether someone should hold a role that depends on setting an example.

In each case, the point concerns the person or how a rule is applied. It still says nothing about whether their argument is sound or strong.
