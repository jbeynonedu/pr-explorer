# Slippery slope

**A slippery slope argument claims that a first, modest step will lead through a chain of consequences or small steps to an extreme and undesirable outcome.** It concludes that the first step should not be taken. It is a fallacy when the links in the chain are not supported, or their likelihood is exaggerated. It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy), sometimes grouped with the fallacies of presumption.

It typically begins with something small and snowballs into something extreme, often with the word "inevitably". It is common in moral and public debate.

## The pattern

```sf
1. If A happens, then B will happen.
2. If B happens, then C will happen.
3. If C happens, then D will happen.
4. D must not happen.
C. Therefore, A must not happen.
```

Read with premise 4 as "not D" and the conclusion as "not A", the form is valid: a [hypothetical syllogism](hypothetical-syllogism) followed by [modus tollens](modus-tollens). The problem is not the form but the premises. In real slippery slopes, each "if... then" is typically at best probable, so the argument has to be judged as an inductive chain.

## Why long chains are weak

Small uncertainties compound, because the chances multiply. Suppose a chain like the one above had four links, from A to E. Suppose also that each step, once reached, has an 80 per cent chance of leading to the next. The chance of going all the way is 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8, about 0.41. The end of the chain is less likely than not, even though every single step looked likely.

## Examples

A student is told, "If you hand in this one assignment late, you'll start missing other deadlines, then you'll fail the subject, then you won't finish school."

```sf
1. If you hand in this assignment late, you will miss other deadlines.
2. If you miss other deadlines, you will fail the subject.
3. If you fail the subject, you will not finish school.
4. You must finish school.
C. Therefore, you must not hand in this assignment late.
```

Handing in work on time is a good idea, but none of the links is supported.

At a council meeting, a resident objects to allowing food trucks in a park on weekends: "First it'll be weekends, then every day. Then the trucks will take over the park, and families will stop coming."

```sf
1. If food trucks are allowed on weekends, they will be allowed every day.
2. If they are allowed every day, they will take over the park.
3. If they take over the park, families will stop coming.
4. Families must not stop coming to the park.
C. Therefore, food trucks must not be allowed on weekends.
```

Each step may be possible, but no reason is given for any of the links.

## Vague-term slippery slopes

Some slippery slopes depend on vague words rather than causes. If adding one more stone never turns scattered stones into a heap, it can seem that adding stones one at a time never could. Each small step looks acceptable, yet the steps lead to a conclusion we reject. The mistake is to assume that because no single step makes a difference, all the steps together cannot.

## When it is not fallacious

Not every argument about a chain of consequences is a fallacy. If each link is well supported by evidence, the argument may be inductively [strong](strong-weak). Whether it is depends on the probability at each step, and on how many steps there are: even well-supported links weaken over a long chain.
