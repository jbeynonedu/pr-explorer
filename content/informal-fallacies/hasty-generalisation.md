# Hasty generalisation

**A hasty generalisation draws a broad conclusion about a group from a sample that is too small, unrepresentative or biased to justify it.** It is a way of jumping to conclusions. It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) of weak induction: the mistake is overreach, not irrelevance.

## The pattern

```sf
1. Some members of group A have property B.
C. Therefore, all or most members of group A have property B.
```

Two elements must both be present:

1. **An inductive inference to a generalisation.** The argument moves from particular cases to a general claim about the group.
2. **Insufficient evidence.** The cases are too few, or not representative, to support a conclusion that strong.

Many stereotypes about groups of people are formed this way, from limited, selected or unrepresentative evidence.

## Examples

After a school excursion, a teacher says, "Two Year 9 students were rude to the bus driver. Year 9s just don't respect adults."

```sf
1. Two Year 9 students were rude to the bus driver.
C. Therefore, most Year 9 students do not respect adults.
```

Two students out of a whole year level is far too small a sample.

A traveller says, "I spent one rainy weekend in Melbourne. It's a cold, wet city."

```sf
1. It was cold and wet during my weekend in Melbourne.
C. Therefore, Melbourne is generally cold and wet.
```

One weekend tells you about that weekend, not about a city's weather across the year. Even if Melbourne often is cold and wet, one weekend does not show it.

A shopper reads ten online reviews of a café, all glowing, and concludes that customers love it. Ten reviews is a small sample, and it may be a biased one. The café might, for example, have asked only its happiest customers to post.

```sf
1. The ten customers who posted online reviews of the café all had an excellent experience.
C. Therefore, most of the café's customers have an excellent experience.
```

## A common mistake

A premise that is *itself* a generalisation is not a hasty generalisation. Consider:

```sf
1. If a policy has increased crime wherever it has been introduced, then it should be abandoned.
2. This policy has increased crime wherever it has been introduced.
C. Therefore, this policy should be abandoned.
```

This is a valid [modus ponens](modus-ponens). Premise 2 is a generalisation, and it may rest on evidence that should be questioned. You can challenge whether it is true. But no generalisation is *drawn in the argument*, so there is no hasty generalisation.

Compare:

```sf
1. In two suburbs, this policy was followed by more crime.
IC1. Therefore, this policy increases crime.
2. If a policy increases crime, then it should be abandoned.
C. Therefore, this policy should be abandoned.
```

Here the step from premise 1 to the intermediate conclusion IC1 is a hasty generalisation: the argument itself leaps from two cases to a general claim.

## When it is not fallacious

Generalising is not a mistake in itself; we make useful generalisations all the time. A [generalisation](generalisation) from a large, representative sample can be [inductively strong](strong-weak). Deciding whether a sample is "enough" is often a judgement call. Read the argument charitably, and name the fallacy only when the gap between the evidence and the conclusion is clear.

## Not to be confused with

[Composition](composition-division), which reasons from the *parts* of something to the *whole*. A hasty generalisation reasons from *some members* of a group to *all or most members*.
