# Faulty analogy

**A faulty analogy is an argument from analogy in which the similarities between the two things compared are not relevant enough to support the conclusion, or relevant differences are ignored.** It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) of weak induction: the premises are relevant in kind, but too weak to support the conclusion.

## The pattern

An argument from [analogy](analogy) reasons from one case to another because they are alike:

```sf
1. A and B are alike in respects r1, r2, r3...
2. A has property Q.
C. Therefore, B has property Q.
```

The argument is faulty when the shared respects have nothing to do with Q, or when A and B differ in a way that does bear on Q.

## Testing an analogy

1. List the similarities the argument relies on.
2. Ask whether those similarities are relevant to the conclusion.
3. Look for differences that are relevant to the conclusion.

An analogy can share many features and still fail, if none of them is relevant, or one relevant difference outweighs them. The [analogy](analogy) entry sets out these criteria more fully.

## Examples

A student argues, "We're allowed to use a calculator in maths exams, so we should be allowed to use a spellchecker in English exams."

```sf
1. Calculators and spellcheckers are both tools that correct routine errors.
2. Calculators are allowed in maths exams.
C. Therefore, spellcheckers should be allowed in English exams.
```

The similarity is real, but there may be a relevant difference: if an English exam is partly testing spelling, a spellchecker does the very thing being assessed. Whether the analogy holds depends on what each exam is designed to test.

A shopper reasons, "This phone and my last phone are both black and the same size. My last phone lasted five years, so this one will too."

```sf
1. This phone and my last phone are alike in colour and size.
2. My last phone lasted five years.
C. Therefore, this phone will last five years.
```

Colour and size have nothing to do with how long a phone lasts. The relevant features, such as build quality and battery, are not compared at all.

A standard example of a faulty analogy: "If a child gets a new toy, he or she will want to play with it; so if a nation gets new weapons, it will want to use them." Playing with a toy is harmless fun; using weapons against other nations is a grave act, usually taken only after long deliberation. The difference undermines the comparison.

## When it is not fallacious

When the similarities are relevant to the conclusion and there are no relevant differences, an argument from analogy can be [inductively strong](strong-weak). Analogies are also used legitimately to explain rather than to argue, by making an unfamiliar idea clearer through a familiar one (see [purposes of argument](purposes-of-argument)). An explanatory analogy is not trying to prove anything, so it is judged by how well it clarifies.

Some important philosophical arguments are arguments from analogy, such as versions of the [teleological argument](teleological-argument). Whether they are faulty is debated, and is judged by the same test.
