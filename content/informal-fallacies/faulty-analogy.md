# Faulty analogy

**A faulty analogy is an argument from analogy in which the similarities between the two things compared are not relevant enough to support the conclusion.** It is also faulty when relevant differences are ignored. It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) of [weak induction](strong-weak). The argument offers the right kind of support for an inductive conclusion, but not enough to make it probable.

## The pattern

An argument from [analogy](analogy) reasons from one case to another because they are alike:

```sf
1. A and B are alike in respects r1, r2, r3...
2. A has property Q.
C. Therefore, B probably has property Q.
```

The argument is faulty when the shared respects have nothing to do with Q. It is also faulty when A and B differ in a way that does bear on Q.

## Testing an analogy

1. Check that A and B really are alike in the ways claimed.
2. Ask whether those similarities are relevant to the conclusion.
3. Look for differences that are relevant to the conclusion.

An analogy can share many features and still fail, if none of them is relevant, or if one relevant difference outweighs them. The [analogy](analogy) entry sets out the criteria more fully.

## Examples

A student argues, "We're allowed calculators in maths exams, so we should be allowed spellcheckers in the English spelling test."

```sf
1. Calculators and spellcheckers are both tools that do routine work (arithmetic, spelling) for the student.
2. Calculators are allowed in maths exams.
C. Therefore, spellcheckers should be allowed in the English spelling test.
```

The similarity is real, but there is a relevant difference. A spelling test exists to assess spelling, so a spellchecker would do the very thing being tested. The analogy is faulty.

A shopper reasons, "This phone and my last phone are both black and the same size. My last phone lasted five years, so this one will too."

```sf
1. This phone and my last phone are alike in colour and size.
2. My last phone lasted five years.
C. Therefore, this phone will last five years.
```

Colour and size have nothing to do with how long a phone lasts. The relevant features, such as build quality and battery, are not compared at all.

One example from the study of fallacies compares toys and weapons:

```sf
1. If a child gets a new toy, the child will want to play with it.
2. A nation with new weapons is like a child with a new toy. (HP)
C. Therefore, if a nation gets new weapons, it will want to use them.
```

A toy is for amusement. Weapons are for inflicting harm, and are usually used only after extensive deliberation. That difference undermines the comparison.

## When it is not fallacious

When the similarities are relevant and there are no relevant differences, an argument from analogy can be [inductively strong](strong-weak).

Not every analogy is an argument. Some simply compare two things, or explain an unfamiliar idea through a familiar one (see [purposes of argument](purposes-of-argument)). An explanatory analogy does not offer evidence for a conclusion, so it is judged by how well it clarifies. It can still mislead if the similarities are remote, or if it is later treated as evidence.

Some important philosophical arguments are arguments from analogy, such as versions of the [teleological argument](teleological-argument). Whether they are faulty is debated, and is judged by the same test.
