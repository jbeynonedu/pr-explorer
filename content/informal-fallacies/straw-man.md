# Straw man

**A straw man fallacy replaces an opponent's actual position with a distorted, weaker version that is easier to attack, then attacks that version as if it were theirs.** It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) of relevance, a form of *ignoratio elenchi* (irrelevant conclusion): even a successful attack on the distorted version does not show that the real one is false. The name evokes the straw figures once used for combat and archery practice, which look like real people but are easily knocked over.

## The pattern

```sf
1. A holds P*. (In fact, A holds P, a different position.)
2. P* is false (or absurd).
C. Therefore, A's position should be rejected.
```

Two things go wrong. A does not hold P*, so premise 1 is false. And even if P* is false, that does not show that P is false.

## Common ways of distorting

- **Exaggerating.** Turning a modest claim into an extreme one.
- **Oversimplifying.** Dropping the qualifications that made the position reasonable.
- **Attributing views the person does not hold.** Treating something remote from their view as if it were their view.

## Examples

At a school council meeting, Priya proposes that the canteen offer at least one vegetarian option each day. Sam replies, "So Priya wants to ban meat from the canteen. That's ridiculous."

```sf
1. Priya wants to ban meat from the canteen.
2. Banning meat from the canteen would be ridiculous.
C. Therefore, Priya's proposal should be rejected.
```

Premise 1 misrepresents Priya. She proposed adding an option, not removing meat. This is exaggeration.

A council report recommends a speed limit of 40 km/h near the school during drop-off and pick-up times. A resident writes, "The council wants everyone to crawl along at 40 all day. Nobody will put up with that."

```sf
1. The council wants a 40 km/h limit at all times.
2. A 40 km/h limit at all times is unacceptable.
C. Therefore, the council's recommendation should be rejected.
```

The resident has dropped the qualification "during drop-off and pick-up times". This is oversimplifying. Premise 1 is false, so the objection misses the actual recommendation.

## When it is not fallacious

- **When the weak version is what the person actually holds.** Criticising a position someone really holds is fair, even if it is easy to criticise. Criticising a weaker version that some supporters hold is also fair, if it is labelled as theirs and not presented as the strongest version.
- **When drawing out a genuine consequence.** Showing that someone's view really does lead to an unwelcome result is a legitimate objection. The question is whether it really follows.

The remedy is the principle of charity: before criticising a position, state it in the strongest form its supporters would accept. If they would not recognise your version as their view, you may be attacking a straw man.
