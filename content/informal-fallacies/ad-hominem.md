# Ad hominem (abusive and circumstantial)

*Attacking the person*

**An ad hominem fallacy rejects an argument or claim by attacking the person who makes it, rather than the argument itself.** *Ad hominem* is Latin for "to the person". It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) of relevance: facts about the arguer are offered as if they bore on whether the conclusion is true.

## Two kinds

**Abusive ad hominem** attacks the person's character, intelligence or conduct.

```sf
1. A argues that P.
2. A has some unfavourable trait.
C. Therefore, P is false (or A's argument should be rejected).
```

**Circumstantial ad hominem** points to the person's circumstances, such as their job, interests or affiliations, and suggests their view comes from self-interest rather than good evidence.

```sf
1. A argues that P.
2. A is in circumstances that give A a reason to argue that P.
C. Therefore, P is false (or A's argument should be rejected).
```

[Tu quoque](tu-quoque), rejecting an argument because the arguer does not act on it, is a special kind of circumstantial ad hominem.

In both patterns, premise 2 may well be true. The problem is that it is irrelevant to whether P is true. Even a biased or self-interested person can put forward a [sound](sound) argument, and that argument stands or falls on its premises and structure.

## Examples

A student council is debating a proposal from Sam to lengthen the lunch break.

```sf
1. Sam argues that the lunch break should be longer.
2. Sam is lazy and never finishes anything.
C. Therefore, the lunch break should not be longer.
```

This is abusive. Even if Sam is lazy, the proposal should be judged on its reasons: for example, whether students have enough time to eat.

The canteen manager reports that the new menu has less sugar than the old one. A parent replies, "Of course she'd say that. She wrote the menu."

```sf
1. The canteen manager claims that the new menu has less sugar.
2. The canteen manager wrote the new menu.
C. Therefore, the new menu does not have less sugar.
```

This is circumstantial. The manager's role does not show that her claim is false; the sugar content can be checked.

## An insult is not yet an argument

Insults are common in heated debate. In a 1991 House of Representatives debate, for example, the Treasurer, Paul Keating, told the Leader of the Opposition, "You disgraceful, disgusting fraud." An insult on its own is a claim about a person, not an argument. It becomes an ad hominem fallacy when it is offered as a reason to reject what the person has argued.

## When it is not fallacious

Facts about a person are relevant when the question is about the person, not about their argument. Someone's conduct may be a good reason not to choose them as a leader, even though it says nothing about the quality of their reasoning on another topic.

Facts about a person can also be relevant when you are accepting a claim only on their word. A strong interest in the outcome can be a reason to check their unsupported claims more carefully, and so it bears on how [credible](credible) they are. It is not a reason to conclude that the claim is false. Accepting a claim because of who makes it raises the matching problem: see [ad verecundiam](ad-verecundiam).

The test is relevance. Ask whether the fact about the person bears on what is actually being decided.
