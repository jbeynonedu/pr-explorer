# Ad hominem (abusive and circumstantial)

*Attacking the person*

**An ad hominem fallacy rejects an argument or claim by attacking the person who makes it, rather than the argument itself.** It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) of relevance: facts about the arguer are offered as if they showed that the conclusion is false or the argument fails.

## Two kinds

**Abusive ad hominem** attacks the person's character, intelligence or conduct.

```sf
1. A argues (or claims) that P.
2. A has some unfavourable trait.
C. Therefore, P is false (or A's argument should be rejected).
```

**Circumstantial ad hominem** points to the person's circumstances, such as their job, position or affiliations, rather than the merits of their argument. A common version suggests their view comes from self-interest rather than good evidence.

```sf
1. A argues (or claims) that P.
2. A is in certain circumstances (for example, A has an interest in P being accepted).
C. Therefore, P is false (or A's argument should be rejected).
```

Abusive attacks target the person themselves (their traits or conduct); circumstantial attacks target the situation they are *in*. [Tu quoque](tu-quoque) is a special kind of circumstantial ad hominem.

In both patterns, premise 2 may well be true. The problem is that it does not show P is false, and it says nothing about whether A's reasons support P. Even a biased or self-interested person can put forward a [sound](sound) (or [strong](strong-weak)) argument; it stands or falls on its own premises and reasoning.

## Examples

A student council is debating Sam's proposal to lengthen the lunch break. Another member says, "We can ignore this. Sam is arrogant and rude to everyone."

```sf
1. Sam argues that the lunch break should be longer.
2. Sam is arrogant and rude to everyone.
C. Therefore, Sam's proposal should be rejected.
```

This is abusive. The proposal should be judged on its reasons, such as whether students have enough time to eat.

The canteen manager says the new menu has less sugar. A parent replies, "Of course she'd say that. She wrote the menu. It's probably not true."

```sf
1. The canteen manager claims that the new menu has less sugar.
2. The canteen manager wrote the new menu.
C. Therefore, the new menu probably does not have less sugar.
```

This is circumstantial. The manager's role does not make her claim false, or even probably false; the sugar content can be checked. Read charitably, "Of course she'd say that" on its own may only mean "Don't just take her word for it". That is a fair request to check, not a fallacy.

## An insult is not yet an argument

In a 1991 House of Representatives debate, Treasurer Paul Keating told the Leader of the Opposition, "You disgraceful, disgusting fraud." The line was part of a longer reply that also gave reasons, so the quotation alone does not show whether it was a fallacy. An insult on its own is a claim about a person, not an argument. It becomes an ad hominem fallacy when it is offered as a reason to reject what the person has argued.

## When it is not fallacious

Facts about a person are relevant when the question is about the person, not their argument. Someone's conduct may be a good reason not to choose them as a leader, though it says nothing about their reasoning on other topics.

They are also relevant when you accept a claim only on someone's word. A strong interest in the outcome bears on how [credible](credible) they are, and is a reason to check their claims more carefully. It is not a reason to conclude that the claim is false. The mirror-image error, accepting a claim merely because of who makes it, is [ad verecundiam](ad-verecundiam).

The test is relevance: does the fact about the person bear on what is being decided?
