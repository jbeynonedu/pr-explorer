# Ad verecundiam (appeal to the wrong authority)

*Appealing to the wrong authority*

**The ad verecundiam fallacy accepts a claim because someone presented as an authority asserts it, when they are not a relevant authority.** It is also committed when an authority's say-so is treated as a substitute for reasons. It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) of relevance.

## The legitimate form

Relying on experts is often reasonable. No one can check everything personally, and much of what we know comes from people with expertise. A good appeal to authority looks like this:

```sf
1. E is an expert in field D.
2. The claim P falls within field D.
3. There is broad agreement among experts in D that P.
C. Therefore, P is probably true.
```

This is not deductively valid: experts can be wrong. But it can be [inductively strong](strong-weak). The authority stands in for reasons you cannot check yourself; it does not replace them.

## The fallacious pattern

```sf
1. E says that P.
C. Therefore, P.
```

where E lacks relevant expertise, or the other conditions of the legitimate form are missing.

## How it goes wrong

- **No relevant expertise.** A celebrity's or sports star's endorsement of a product or cause is taken as a reason to accept it.
- **Outside the field.** A genuine expert's say-so is relied on for a claim outside their field.
- **Divided experts.** When experts disagree, it is an error to rely on the authority of only some of them.
- **Untrustworthy or misreported.** The authority has a strong reason to mislead and this is ignored, or their words are misquoted.
- **Unnamed authority.** "Experts say", with no indication of who, gives no way to check premises 1 to 3 of the legitimate form.

## Examples

A television advertisement shows a famous swimmer saying, "This is the best air conditioner on the market."

```sf
1. A famous swimmer says this is the best air conditioner.
C. Therefore, this is the best air conditioner.
```

Swimming expertise gives no special knowledge of air conditioners. Premise 1 gives no support to the conclusion.

A post shared online says, "A university professor of engineering says this diet cures headaches."

```sf
1. A professor of engineering says this diet cures headaches.
C. Therefore, this diet cures headaches.
```

The professor is an authority, but not on medicine. Premise 2 of the legitimate form fails.

Historians still dispute what caused a certain event. In a debate, a student says, "A leading historian says it was caused by drought, so that settles it."

```sf
1. A leading historian says the event was caused by drought.
C. Therefore, the event was caused by drought.
```

The historian is a relevant expert, but historians are divided, so premise 3 of the legitimate form fails. Citing one side of a dispute cannot settle it; treating it as settled is the fallacy.

## When it is not fallacious

When the conditions in the legitimate form are met, and the authority is reported accurately and has no strong reason to mislead, appealing to authority is usually reasonable. Most everyday appeals to expertise are of this kind. Courts, for example, could hardly work without expert testimony. Even then, the conclusion is probable rather than certain, and it can be challenged with better evidence. See [credible](credible).

Accepting a claim merely because of who says it is the mirror image of [ad hominem](ad-hominem), which rejects a claim merely because of who says it. Accepting a claim because many people believe it is [ad populum](ad-populum).
