# Ad misericordiam (illicit appeal to emotion)

*Appeal to pity*

**The ad misericordiam fallacy tries to win acceptance for a conclusion by arousing emotion, where the emotion is not a relevant reason for that conclusion.** The name means "appeal to pity", and pity is the central case. The same mistake can be made with other emotions, such as fear or pride. Strictly, *ad misericordiam* names the appeal to pity; "illicit appeal to emotion" names the wider family. It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy) of relevance.

## The pattern

```sf
1. [A situation that arouses pity, fear or another emotion.]
C. Therefore, P.
```

where the situation, however moving, has no bearing on whether P is true or should be done.

The emotion may be entirely appropriate. The mistake is to treat it as evidence for a conclusion it does not bear on. Sympathy alone is generally not evidence for believing that a claim is true.

To check: what emotion is being aroused? Does the situation that arouses it bear on this conclusion? When evaluating an argument, set the emotional appeal aside and ask what reasons remain.

## Examples

A student who has submitted a weak essay emails the teacher: "I've had a terrible week. My grandmother has been sick and I've hardly slept. Please give this essay an A."

```sf
1. I have had a terrible week.
2. A student who has had a terrible week should receive a higher mark. (HP)
C. Therefore, you should give this essay an A.
```

The student's week may well deserve sympathy, and it might be a good reason to grant an extension. But the mark should reflect the quality of the essay, which the week does not change. The hidden premise is where the argument fails.

A home-security advertisement shows a family asleep while a shadowy figure tries the front door. The voice-over says, "Can you really afford to be without us?"

```sf
1. [Images of a break-in at night.]
C. Therefore, you should buy this security system.
```

This is an appeal to fear. Whether to buy depends on things such as the actual risk to your home, how well the system reduces it, and its cost. The advertisement gives no information about any of these. (When the person arguing is the source of the threat, as in "agree, or face the consequences", the fallacy is usually called *ad baculum*, the appeal to force.)

## When it is not fallacious

Appeals to emotion are not always fallacious.

- **When the emotion tracks a relevant fact.** In deciding *what to do*, someone's suffering is often a genuine reason. Pity for people affected by a disaster is a fair reason to donate. Appeals of this kind belong naturally to practical reasoning, about how to act. Fallacy is more likely when they are used to support a belief about what is true. Even in decisions about what to do, the emotion must bear on that particular decision: hardship may justify an extension, but not a higher mark.
- **When emotion accompanies good reasons rather than replacing them.** There is nothing wrong with using emotion in an argument, provided it is not doing the work that evidence should do. A speech can be moving and well argued at once.

The test is whether the emotion is offered *instead of* reasons for this particular conclusion. Persuading someone is not the same as giving them good reasons: see [justification and persuasion](justification-persuasion).
