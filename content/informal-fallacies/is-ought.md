# Is-ought fallacy

*Deriving "ought" from "is"*

**The is-ought fallacy draws a normative conclusion, about what ought to be, from premises that only describe what is, with no normative premise to bridge the gap.** It turns on the difference between [descriptive and normative claims](descriptive-normative). It is an [informal fallacy](informal-fallacy).

## Hume's observation

The problem is usually traced to David Hume (1711–1776), in *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–1740):

> In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remarked, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning [...] when of a sudden I am surprised to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as this ought, or ought not, expresses some new relation or affirmation, it is necessary that it should be observed and explained; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it.

*Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 1, Section 1. Standard Ebooks edition, based on Project Gutenberg (italics in the original not reproduced).*

In other words: every system of morality Hume had read moves from statements about what *is* the case to statements about what *ought* to be, without noticing. Hume says that move needs to be explained. An "ought" says something new, and it seems inconceivable how it could follow from premises that contain no "ought".

## The pattern

```sf
1. [Descriptive premise(s) only.]
C. Therefore, [normative conclusion].
```

## Examples

A student says, "The library is the quietest place in the school, so you should study there."

```sf
1. The library is the quietest place in the school.
C. Therefore, you should study there.
```

The premise describes the library. The conclusion says what you should do. As it stands, nothing connects them.

A friend says, "This medicine relieves headaches, so you ought to take it."

```sf
1. This medicine relieves headaches.
C. Therefore, you ought to take it.
```

The conclusion depends on more than the fact about the medicine: for example, whether you have a headache, and whether you ought to relieve it this way.

## Repairing the gap, and when it is not fallacious

Often the missing normative premise is simply unstated. Once it is written in as a [hidden premise](hidden-premise), the argument is valid:

```sf
1. The library is the quietest place in the school.
2. If a place is the quietest place in the school, you ought to study there. (HP)
C. Therefore, you ought to study in the library.
```

The hidden premise is now the part to evaluate: is quietness really what matters most when choosing where to study? An argument that states or clearly assumes a normative premise does not commit the fallacy. Whether that premise is true is a separate question. The skill is to find the hidden "ought" and evaluate it.

## A contested principle

How Hume's paragraph should be read is disputed. On one influential reading, it states a rule of logic, which some philosophers call "Hume's Law": no "ought" can validly follow from purely factual premises. Other readers think Hume meant something narrower: that reason alone cannot first discover moral qualities, which takes feeling (sentiment). They point out that Hume himself often reasons from facts to moral conclusions.

Some philosophers deny the principle outright. John Searle (1932–2025) argued in 1964 that an obligation can be derived from facts about a promise being made. His argument relies on the institution of promising. Others, such as Antony Flew (1923–2010), defended the principle against Searle's argument. The debate continues.

## Not to be confused with

- The [illicit appeal to nature](appeal-to-nature), which concludes that something is good because it is natural. It is often one case of the is-ought fallacy.
- G. E. Moore's (1873–1958) "naturalistic fallacy", which concerns whether "good" can be defined in terms of natural properties. The term is sometimes used more loosely for the is-ought fallacy itself, but Moore's own point concerns definition, not inference.
