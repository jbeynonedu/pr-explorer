# Limitations of deduction

**Deduction can guarantee a conclusion only if its premises are true, and the truth of those premises usually has to be established some other way.** A [sound](sound) deductive argument guarantees that its conclusion is true. But whether an argument is sound depends on premises that deduction alone does not supply.

## Validity is not truth

[Validity](valid) rules out true premises with a false conclusion. It does not rule out a false conclusion. A valid argument can have a false premise and a false conclusion:

```sf
1. Oranges are either fruit or musical instruments.
2. Oranges are not fruit.
C. Therefore, oranges are musical instruments.
```

So to rebut an argument, you can show that it is invalid, or that a premise is false. Logic can help with the first. The second usually needs evidence about the world.

## Premises often come from induction

Many premises in deductive arguments are themselves the result of [inductive](induction) reasoning:

```sf
1. If our local high school is a great school, then we should enrol our children there.
2. Our local high school is a great school.
C. Therefore, we should enrol our children at our local high school.
```

The argument is valid. But premise 2 would be supported, if at all, by evidence: results, reports, experience. That support is inductive, so it makes the premise probable rather than certain. A deductive argument resting on such a premise is only as secure as the induction behind it. This links the limitations of deduction to those of induction, raised most famously by David Hume (1711–1776): see [Hume's problem of induction](problem-of-induction).

## Does deduction add information?

The conclusion of a *valid* deductive argument is often described as already "contained" in its premises. On this description, it makes explicit what they say rather than adding new information. That is one common way of explaining why true premises guarantee it. (Some philosophers question how well the "contained" metaphor fits every valid argument.)

This does not make deduction trivial. What the premises contain may be far from obvious. A mathematical proof can derive a result such as Pythagoras' theorem from simple facts about the areas of squares and triangles.

## Why it matters

None of this means deduction is weaker than induction. Each does a different job. Mathematics relies mainly on deduction; building knowledge of the world relies heavily on induction; science combines them (see the [hypothetico-deductive method](hypothetico-deductive)). In many evaluations, the harder work is deciding whether the premises are true.
