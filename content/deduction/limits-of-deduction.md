# Limitations of deduction

**Deduction can guarantee a conclusion only if its premises are true, and deduction itself cannot show that they are.** A [sound](sound) deductive argument establishes its conclusion beyond doubt, but that certainty depends on premises that usually have to be established some other way.

## Validity is not truth

[Validity](valid) rules out true premises with a false conclusion. It does not rule out a false conclusion. A valid argument can have false premises and a false conclusion:

```sf
1. If the moon is made of cheese, then aliens exist.
2. The moon is made of cheese.
C. Therefore, aliens exist.
```

So to rebut an argument, you can show that it is invalid, or that a premise is false. Logic can help with the first. The second usually needs evidence about the world.

## Premises often come from induction

Many premises in deductive arguments are themselves the result of [inductive](induction) reasoning:

```sf
1. If Banksia College is a great school, then we should enrol our children there.
2. Banksia College is a great school.
C. Therefore, we should enrol our children at Banksia College.
```

The argument is valid. But premise 2 would be supported, if at all, by evidence: results, reports, experience. That support is inductive, so it makes the premise probable rather than certain. A deductive argument resting on such a premise is only as secure as the induction behind it, which links its limitations to [Hume's problem of induction](problem-of-induction).

## No new information

A deductive conclusion makes explicit what the premises already contain; it does not add information that goes beyond them. That is the source of its certainty: if the conclusion went beyond the premises, the premises could be true and the conclusion false.

This does not make deduction trivial. What the premises contain may not be obvious. A mathematical proof can derive a result that nobody would have noticed in the premises, such as Pythagoras' theorem, which can be proved from simple facts about the areas of squares and triangles.

## Why it matters

None of this means deduction is weaker than induction. Each does a different job. Mathematics relies mainly on deduction; building knowledge of the world relies heavily on induction; science combines them (see the [hypothetico-deductive method](hypothetico-deductive)). When you evaluate a deductive argument, its validity is usually the easy part; the real work is deciding whether its premises are true.
