## bland-reason-ch1-sf

> When we analyse something complex, we break it down into simpler parts. In logic and philosophy, one way to analyse an argument is to deconstruct it into standard form. We do this by identifying the conclusion of the argument (the factual statement that is asserted) and the premises (the reasons given in support of the conclusion), and laying these out in a logical structure. (p. 13)

> We can represent this argument in standard form by numbering the premises and writing the conclusion last (p. 13)
. Penny's argument (p. 13): `1. Australians lose more money per person to gambling than any other country.` `2. Children are especially susceptible to gambling advertisements.` `C. Therefore, the federal government should ban TV advertisements for gambling companies.` (Real-world premise 1: verify independently if used, or use a fresh example.)
> Occasionally you may wish to develop a more complex argument that uses multiple chains of reasoning. If this is the case, you can insert intermediate conclusions, which can then be used to support the argument’s main conclusion. These are typically notated with “IC” in standard form. For example, consider the following argument: In this argument, we reach intermediate conclusion 1 (IC1) from premises 1 and 2, and we reach intermediate conclusion 2 (IC2) from premises 3 and 4. The main conclusion of the argument is simply a conjunction of IC1 and IC2. The important thing to note here is that the intermediate conclusions are drawn purely from the premises in the argument – they add no additional new information. (p. 15)

> A hidden premise, otherwise known as a tacit premise, is an assumption in an argument that is not explicitly stated but is necessary for the argument to work. Often, hidden premises are unstated because they are considered obvious or contain widely shared and agreed knowledge, but occasionally these hidden premises are more controversial and are subject to challenge. When converting a prose argument to standard form, it is important to be able to identify hidden premises and then include these premises in the standard form construction, allowing the truth value of such premises to be discussed and evaluated. (p. 16)


## bland-reason-ch3-mistakes
Bland, *Reason* (Revised Edition), The Glennie School, Oct 2026. CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file `Reason Revised Edition.pdf`; text copied from the Project file by script (whitespace normalised).
> To avoid this problem, remove any author or philosopher attribution from the argument itself, unless that attribution is the very point being examined. A clearer version of the argument would read: 1. If an action results in the greatest happiness for the greatest number, then the action is morally correct. 2. Capital punishment results in the greatest happiness for the greatest number. C. Therefore, capital punishment is morally correct. In your evaluation of this argument, you may (and should) attribute the principle expressed in Premise 1 – known as the greatest happiness principle – to philosophers such as Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill. You can then go on to discuss potential objections to this principle and their rebuttals. But when it comes to the logical formulation of the argument itself, it’s best to leave the philosopher’s name out. Your premises should make clear, evaluable claims – not merely cite beliefs. (p. 68)

> To preserve the links between the premises and conclusion of a deductive argument, it is very important that the wording of propositions is as precise and consistent as possible. Consider the following: Reason Page 69 1. If a person is compassionate, then they donate to charity. 2. Maria does not give money to charity. C. Therefore, Maria is not compassionate. Again, this argument appears to take the form of modus tollens13, which we know is a valid form. But again, there is a flaw: the first premise refers to donating to charity and the second premise refers to giving money to charity, which may not mean the same thing. Maria may volunteer her time, for example, which may still be considered as donating to charity. Because the wording of the propositions doesn’t match exactly, the argument is not deductively valid. (pp. 68–69)


## syllabus-p7
QCAA, *Philosophy & Reason 2025 v1.3*, January 2026. CC BY 4.0, © State of Queensland (QCAA) 2026 www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/copyright. Project file.

> When examining the reasoning of others, the deconstruction of arguments is a key tool of interpretation and analysis. This involves breaking down arguments into their constituent components of premise/s and conclusion/s, including assumed premises necessary for an argument to cohere. The structured setting out of this information in standard form is a feature of philosophical analysis. It provides a basis for the evaluation of both the inferential quality of an argument, and the truth of its premises. (p. 7)

> When creating responses in Philosophy & Reason, an analytical essay is connected prose that has the purpose of establishing a claim or assertion (central thesis) through careful reasoning. In this respect, essays present an argument. As part of this process, key concepts, arguments and assumptions are clarified, and counterarguments identified and responded to, applying the principle of charity. (p. 7)

## forallx-calgary-ch1

> Many arguments start with premises, and end with a conclusion, but not all of them. (ch. 1)

Examples given: conclusion first ("The gardener did it. After all, it was either the butler or the gardener. And the butler didn't do it."), conclusion in the middle; one sentence containing a whole argument ("The butler has an alibi; so they cannot have done it.").

> As a guide, these words are often used to indicate an argument's conclusion: so, therefore, hence, thus, accordingly, consequently. For this reason, they are sometimes called conclusion indicator words. (ch. 1)

> By contrast, these expressions are premise indicator words, as they often indicate that we are dealing with a premise, rather than a conclusion: since, because, given that. But in analysing an argument, there is no substitute for a good nose. (ch. 1)
