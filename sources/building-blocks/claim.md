## bland-reason-ch1-claim
Bland, *Reason* (Revised Edition), The Glennie School, Oct 2026. CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file `Reason Revised Edition.pdf`; text copied from the Project file by script (whitespace normalised).
> Before we move on, it’s important to understand the distinction between an argument and a claim. As you have just read, an argument is the establishment of a conclusion which is supported by reasons for believing the truth of that conclusion (premises). In other words, when we make an argument, we have to give reasons or evidence in support of the conclusion. On the other hand, a claim is just a statement that asserts something to be true or false – it’s like a conclusion without any premises. From a philosophical point of view, a claim carries little weight in terms of rigor or persuasiveness. Anybody can make a claim about anything, but without evidence and reasoning, a claim lacks justification and is open to rejection. Claims can be a fascinating starting point for a broader philosophical discussion (for example, consider the claim that “humans lack free will”, and discuss the implications that this may have), however they cannot function in place of an argument. (p. 14)

> Sometimes you may find claims within the premises of an argument. In this case, they can be useful for supporting a particular conclusion, but are themselves open to challenge in an evaluation. For example, consider the previous example we looked at: It’s fair to say that both premises 1 and 2 are claims. If we were constructing a more detailed argument in support of the conclusion, we would need to provide evidence to support these premises. This evidence doesn’t need to be included in our standard-form argument, but it should be present somewhere. The key tenet of argument and critical reasoning is that we can’t just make statements without evidence or reasoning – we have to provide as much support as possible for every claim that we make. (p. 14)

Note (John, 25 Nov): entry uses "a claim on its own gives no reason to accept it", not "carries little weight". Claim and proposition treated as near-synonyms (John, 25 Nov, proposition brief).

## syllabus-p7
QCAA, *Philosophy & Reason 2025 v1.3*, January 2026. CC BY 4.0, © State of Queensland (QCAA) 2026 www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/copyright. Project file.

> When examining the reasoning of others, the deconstruction of arguments is a key tool of interpretation and analysis. This involves breaking down arguments into their constituent components of premise/s and conclusion/s, including assumed premises necessary for an argument to cohere. The structured setting out of this information in standard form is a feature of philosophical analysis. It provides a basis for the evaluation of both the inferential quality of an argument, and the truth of its premises. (p. 7)

> When creating responses in Philosophy & Reason, an analytical essay is connected prose that has the purpose of establishing a claim or assertion (central thesis) through careful reasoning. In this respect, essays present an argument. As part of this process, key concepts, arguments and assumptions are clarified, and counterarguments identified and responded to, applying the principle of charity. (p. 7)

Also syllabus p. 10 (Unit 1 description): "To evaluate an argument is to assess a series of truth claims."
