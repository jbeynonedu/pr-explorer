# Source pack: proposition

## bland-reason-ch1
Bland, *Reason* (Revised Edition), ch. 1, §1.1 "Propositions and Non-Propositions", pp. 9–10 (Exercise 1.1 p. 12). CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file `Reason Revised Edition.pdf`; text copied from the Project file.

> In logic, a proposition is any declarative statement that is capable of holding truth value. In classical logic, the branch that we are focused on in this course, propositions adhere to the principles of bivalence (every proposition has exactly one truth value) and non-contradiction (a proposition cannot be both true and false simultaneously). (p. 9)

> In classical logic, a proposition is a declarative statement that is either true or false. (p. 9)

Bland's examples (p. 9): "(1) Today is Tuesday." "(2) The Prime Minister of our country is Anthony Albanese." "(3) Christmas Day occurs on December 25." "(4) Capital punishment should be abolished."
Bland's notes on them: (1) true at some times, not others; (2) true in Australia, false in the UK; (3) true for everyone; (4) considered true by some, false by others.

Non-propositions (pp. 9–10, table): exclamations (p. 10: "Exclamations are expressions of emotion – they are not capable of being true"; example "Oh, what a beautiful morning!"); questions ("requests for information – they are not capable of being true or false in themselves"); commands ("attempts to elicit action – they are not capable of being true or false in themselves"). Exercise 1.1 (p. 12) adds exclamations ("Hooray for the winning team!", "Wow, that's amazing!") and a fragment ("Because it was raining.").

Truth-value box (p. 9): "the whole notion of 'truth' is deeply contested. [...] For now, we will consider a proposition to be "true" if it corresponds to or matches with a fact or reality."

Fuzzy logic note (p. 9): some modern systems "allow propositions to assume any truth value (typically measured on a numerical scale from 0 to 1)".

Pp. 10–11: descriptive vs normative; analytic vs synthetic (linked, not covered here).

## bland-reason-ch2-dictionary
Bland, ch. 2–3: propositions represented by single letters using a "dictionary" (p. 36, §3.1: "a list of all propositions in an argument and the letters that you are using to represent them"). Example dictionary (p. 27): L = "I win the lotto." W = "I am wealthy."

## sep-structured-propositions (checking only; in copyright, no quotation in entry)
King, J. C., "Structured Propositions", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, substantive revision 10 Dec 2024. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/propositions-structured/
Supports: two speakers can say the same thing with different sentences, in the same or different languages ("Snow is white" / "Schnee ist weiss"); the proposition is what is in the first instance true or false; a declarative sentence is true or false derivatively, by expressing a proposition.

## sep-moral-cognitivism (checking only)
van Roojen, M., "Moral Cognitivism vs. Non-Cognitivism", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-cognitivism/ (search result text, 25 Nov 2026).
Supports: non-cognitivists think moral statements have no substantial truth conditions; cognitivism holds that moral statements express beliefs and are apt for truth and falsity. Used to qualify Bland's example (4).
