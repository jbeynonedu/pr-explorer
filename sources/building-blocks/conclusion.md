# Source pack: conclusion

## bland-reason-ch1-conclusion
Bland, *Reason* (Revised Edition), The Glennie School, Oct 2026. CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file `Reason Revised Edition.pdf`; text copied from the Project file by script (whitespace normalised).
> However, in Philosophy and Reason, an argument means something else entirely – it is the establishment of a factual statement that is supported by evidence or reason. The factual statement that you are trying to assert is called the conclusion, and the reasons that you give for supporting the conclusion are known as premises. (p. 8)

> Occasionally you may wish to develop a more complex argument that uses multiple chains of reasoning. If this is the case, you can insert intermediate conclusions, which can then be used to support the argument’s main conclusion. These are typically notated with “IC” in standard form. For example, consider the following argument: In this argument, we reach intermediate conclusion 1 (IC1) from premises 1 and 2, and we reach intermediate conclusion 2 (IC2) from premises 3 and 4. The main conclusion of the argument is simply a conjunction of IC1 and IC2. The important thing to note here is that the intermediate conclusions are drawn purely from the premises in the argument – they add no additional new information. (p. 15)

Bland's example (p. 15): `1. If it is the weekend, then the public pool will be open.` `2. It is the weekend.` `IC1. Therefore, the public pool will be open.` `3. If it is sunny, then I will go for a swim.` `4. It is sunny.` `IC2. Therefore, I will go for a swim.` `C. Therefore, the public pool will be open and I will go for a swim.`
> Sometimes, you may encounter an argument where the premises are clearly laid out but the conclusion is not directly stated, rather it is inferred or implied. In this case, the argument can be said to contain a hidden conclusion (HC). Be careful with these – sometimes the implied conclusion is obvious, but other times it is vague. It’s always good practice to state a conclusion clearly and explicitly rather than have your listeners jump to the conclusion themselves. However, if you do encounter an argument with a hidden conclusion, do your best to identify what it is and include it in your standard form. (p. 16)

## forallx-calgary-ch1
P. D. Magnus, Tim Button, Robert Trueman, Richard Zach, *forall x: Calgary*, Fall 2025 edition, ch. 1 "Arguments". CC BY 4.0. Text copied from the LaTeX source, github.com/rzach/forallx-yyc, file `forallx-yyc-what.tex`, commit bb86fe9 (22 Oct 2026). HTML: https://forallx.openlogicproject.org/html/Ch1.html

> As a guide, these words are often used to indicate an argument's conclusion: so, therefore, hence, thus, accordingly, consequently. For this reason, they are sometimes called conclusion indicator words. (ch. 1)

> Many arguments start with premises, and end with a conclusion, but not all of them. (ch. 1)

Examples given: conclusion first ("The gardener did it. After all, it was either the butler or the gardener. And the butler didn't do it."), conclusion in the middle; one sentence containing a whole argument ("The butler has an alibi; so they cannot have done it.").
