# Source pack: argument

## bland-reason-ch1-argument
Bland, *Reason* (Revised Edition), The Glennie School, Oct 2026. CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file `Reason Revised Edition.pdf`; text copied from the Project file by script (whitespace normalised).
> However, in Philosophy and Reason, an argument means something else entirely – it is the establishment of a factual statement that is supported by evidence or reason. The factual statement that you are trying to assert is called the conclusion, and the reasons that you give for supporting the conclusion are known as premises. (p. 8)

Note (John, 25 Nov): "factual" dropped in the entry. Bland's own later wording, p. 14, has no "factual": "an argument is the establishment of a conclusion which is supported by reasons for believing the truth of that conclusion (premises)."
> One important characteristic of a deductive argument that sets it apart from other argument types is that the premises of the argument must be linked through a logical process to support the conclusion. Each premise must contribute in some way. (p. 17)

## forallx-calgary-ch1
P. D. Magnus, Tim Button, Robert Trueman, Richard Zach, *forall x: Calgary*, Fall 2025 edition, ch. 1 "Arguments". CC BY 4.0. Text copied from the LaTeX source, github.com/rzach/forallx-yyc, file `forallx-yyc-what.tex`, commit bb86fe9 (22 Oct 2026). HTML: https://forallx.openlogicproject.org/html/Ch1.html
Page numbers from the Fall 2025 PDF supplied by John (`forallxyyc.pdf`, built 23 Oct 2026); text checked against it 25 Nov 2026.

> We will say that an argument is any collection of premises, together with a conclusion. (ch. 1, p. 3)

> In everyday language, we sometimes use the word 'argument' to talk about belligerent shouting matches. [...] They are not arguments, in our sense; they are just disagreements. (ch. 1, p. 2)

> If the premises are true and the argument is a good one, then you have a reason to accept the conclusion. (ch. 1, §1.1, p. 4)

Supports "a bad argument is still an argument": the definition counts any collection of premises with a conclusion; whether it is good is a further question (ch. 1 opening: logic is used in "sorting the good from the bad").

## syllabus-p7
QCAA, *Philosophy & Reason 2025 v1.3*, January 2026. CC BY 4.0, © State of Queensland (QCAA) 2026 www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/copyright. Project file.

> When examining the reasoning of others, the deconstruction of arguments is a key tool of interpretation and analysis. This involves breaking down arguments into their constituent components of premise/s and conclusion/s, including assumed premises necessary for an argument to cohere. The structured setting out of this information in standard form is a feature of philosophical analysis. It provides a basis for the evaluation of both the inferential quality of an argument, and the truth of its premises. (p. 7)

> When creating responses in Philosophy & Reason, an analytical essay is connected prose that has the purpose of establishing a claim or assertion (central thesis) through careful reasoning. In this respect, essays present an argument. As part of this process, key concepts, arguments and assumptions are clarified, and counterarguments identified and responded to, applying the principle of charity. (p. 7)

## forallx-calgary-ch1-butler
forall x: Calgary, ch. 1, pp. 2–3 (LaTeX source `forallx-yyc-what.tex`, commit bb86fe9; Fall 2025 PDF). CC BY 4.0.
Argument (p. 2): "Either the butler or the gardener did it." "The butler didn't do it." "∴ The gardener did it."
> Many arguments start with premises, and end with a conclusion, but not all of them. (p. 3)
Conclusion first (p. 3): "The gardener did it. After all, it was either the butler or the gardener. And the butler didn't do it."
Conclusion in the middle (p. 3): "The butler didn't do it. Accordingly, it was the gardener, given that it was either the gardener or the butler."
Entry's first passage renders the p. 2 argument as prose ("Therefore," for ∴): adapted.
