> Inductive Arguments (p. 19)
> Rather than attempt to guarantee the truth of a conclusion definitively, an inductive argument aims to (p. 19)
> provide probable support for the conclusion. This is the fundamental distinction between the two argument (p. 19)
> types: a deductive argument seeks to guarantee its conclusion — if the premises are true, the conclusion (p. 19)
> must also be true — whereas an inductive argument seeks only to make its conclusion probable. Because of (p. 19)
> this difference, criteria such as validity and soundness do not apply to inductive arguments — rather, these (p. 19)
> arguments can be assessed as being either strong or weak overall. Unlike black-and-white deductive (p. 19)
> arguments, inductive arguments can be thought of as having shades of grey. It is perfectly acceptable to say (p. 19)
> that one inductive argument is stronger or weaker than another inductive argument — ultimately this (p. 19)
> judgment will depend on how well the premises provide support for accepting the conclusion.3 (p. 19)

> probabilistic nature of the inferential link between the premises and conclusion: regardless of the specific (p. 20)

> 2. P has been observed to have an additional property, x. (p. 94)

> the following factors should be considered: (p. 94)
> 1. Truth – check to ensure that the two objects that are being compared are in fact similar in the way (p. 94)
> described. (p. 94)
> 2. Relevance – check to ensure that the shared properties (a, b, c, d, etc) are relevant to the property x (p. 94)

> argument. (p. 94)
> 3. Number – if there are many shared properties (a, b, c, d, etc) between the two entities, then this (p. 94)

> Damer: “One who presents an argument for or against a position should provide reasons that are likely to (p. 103)
> be accepted by a mature, rational person and that meet standard criteria of acceptability.” (p. 33) (p. 103)
> 1. A premise should be considered ‘acceptable’ if it expresses any of the following and none of the (p. 103)
> conditions for unacceptability: (p. 103)
> • A claim that is a matter of undisputed common knowledge. (p. 103)
> • A claim that is confirmed by one’s own personal experience or observation. (p. 103)
> • A claim that is adequately defended in the context of the argument or at least is capable of being (p. 103)
> adequately defended by some other accessible source. (p. 103)
> • An uncontroverted eyewitness testimony, or an uncontroverted claim from a relevant authority. (p. 103)
> • The conclusion of another good argument. (p. 103)
> • A relatively minor claim that seems to be a reasonable assumption in the context of the (p. 103)
> argument. (p. 103)
> 2. A premise should be considered ‘unacceptable’ if it meets any of the following criteria: (p. 103)
> • A claim that contradicts credible evidence, a well-established claim, or a legitimate authority. (p. 103)
> • A claim that is inconsistent with one’s own experiences or observations. (p. 103)
> • A questionable claim that is not adequately defended in the context of the argument or not (p. 103)
> capable of being adequately defended by evidence in some other accessible source. (p. 103)
> • A claim that is self-contradictory or linguistically confusing. (p. 103)
> • A claim that is based on another unstated but highly questionable assumption. (p. 103)
> Criterion 4 – Sufficiency (p. 103)
> Damer: “One who presents an argument for or against a position should attempt to provide relevant and (p. 103)
> acceptable reasons of the right kind, that together are sufficient in number and weight to justify the (p. 103)
> acceptance of the conclusion.” (p. 36) (p. 103)
> 1. Are the reasons provided enough to substantiate the conclusion? (p. 103)
> • If not, then the premises can be said to be insufficient. (p. 103)
> 2. Is the premise based on insufficient evidence or faulty causal analysis? (p. 103)
> • If so, evidence for the premise can be said to be insufficient. (p. 103)

> • Check for tacit premises. If there is a faulty assumption in the tacit premise, this can lead to (p. 103)
