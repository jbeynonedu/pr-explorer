## Points for the entry
- SUMMARY: The concept today is used for a genus of social practices; paradigm cases include Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Confucianism, Daoism (opening paragraph).
- SUMMARY: The word comes from Latin *religio*, roughly "scrupulousness"; it did not originally name a social genus (§1).
- SUMMARY (§1): Three widening "substantive" definitions: theistic (Edward Herbert, 1583–1648, five Common Notions beginning with belief in a supreme deity); polytheistic/spiritual (Edward Burnett Tylor, 1832–1917: "belief in spiritual beings", 1871); cosmic (William James, 1902: "unseen order"; Clifford Geertz, 1973: "a general order of existence"), which includes non-theistic forms of Buddhism, Daoism, karma.
- EXACT (§1): "This third expansion is why non-theistic forms of Buddhism, excluded by the Herbert’s and Tylor’s definitions but today widely considered religions, can serve as “a litmus test” for definitions of the concept"
- EXACT (§1): "Whether a common element can be identified that will coherently ground a substantive definition of “religion” is not a settled question."
- SUMMARY (§1): "Functional" definitions define religion by the role it plays: Durkheim (1912), the practices that unite people into a single moral community; Tillich (1957), whatever dominant concern organises a person's values. Functional definitions can count capitalism, nationalism and Marxism as religions; substantive and functional definitions can give non-overlapping results.
- SUMMARY (§2): Monothetic definitions give necessary and sufficient conditions; polythetic (family-resemblance, after Wittgenstein 1953) definitions require enough of a set of characteristics, none essential. SEP's illustrative set: (A) belief in superempirical beings or powers, (B) ethical norms, (C) worship rituals, (D) participation believed to bestow benefits, (E) participants see themselves as a distinct community.
- SUMMARY (§2): Fides / fiducia / fidelitas (belief, trust, loyalty); "creed, cult, code"; a fourth C, community.
- SUMMARY (§3): Some scholars (e.g. J. Z. Smith 1982, Asad 1993) critique the concept as shaped by Christian, modern and colonial assumptions; more radical critics (e.g. Fitzgerald 2000) deny it refers to anything; realists reply that religion as a social reality exists whether or not a culture has the concept.
