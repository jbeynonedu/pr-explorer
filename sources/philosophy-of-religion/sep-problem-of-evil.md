## Deductive formulation (§1.1), EXACT
> 1. If God exists, then God is omnipotent, omniscient, and morally perfect.
> 2. If God is omnipotent, then God has the power to eliminate all evil.
> 3. If God is omniscient, then God knows when evil exists.
> 4. If God is morally perfect, then God has the desire to eliminate all evil.
> 5. Evil exists.
> 6. If evil exists and God exists, then either God doesn’t have the power to eliminate all evil, or doesn’t know when evil exists, or doesn’t have the desire to eliminate all evil.
> 7. Therefore, God doesn’t exist.
- EXACT: "That this argument is valid is perhaps most easily seen by a reductio argument" (§1.1). "Whether the argument is sound is, of course, a further question, for it may be that one or more of the premises is false." (§1.1)

## For `benevolence` (EXACT, §1.3 / §2)
- Premise (4) is questioned: "It seems possible, then, that there might be evils that are logically necessary for goods that outweigh them, and this possibility provides a reason, accordingly, for questioning one of the premises in the argument set out earlier—namely, premise (4), where it is claimed that if God is morally perfect, then God has the desire to eliminate all evil."
- The more modest premise: an omniscient and morally perfect person "will prevent the existence of such evils if that can be done without either allowing equal or greater evils, or preventing equal or greater goods." (§2)
- Free will: "The good of libertarian free will requires, in short, the possibility of moral evil." (§1.3)

## SUMMARY
- §1.1: Two broad concepts of God: metaphysical (prime mover, first cause, necessary being, ground of being) and religiously-connected (powerful, knowledgeable, morally good person). Evil is a problem for the second, and for the first only if such a being has great power, knowledge and goodness.
- §1.2: Incompatibility (deductive/logical) vs evidential (inductive/probabilistic) formulations.
- §1.3: Abstract vs concrete formulations; details of distribution (children, animals) matter. Plantinga's work began from Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence" (1955); McCloskey (1960) and Aiken (1957–58) also defended incompatibility versions.
- §3.2: Rowe's direct inductive argument: E1 (a fawn dying slowly after a forest fire) and E2 (a murdered girl); inference from (P) "No good that we know of has J" to (Q) "No good has J"; Plantinga's criticism and Rowe's reply. Draper (1989) and Hume (*Dialogues* XI) give indirect (best explanation) versions.
- §4: Responses classified as total refutations, defences and theodicies; "theodicy" in Plantinga and Adams's stronger sense = God's actual reasons; a defence shows reasons may exist without saying what they are.
- §5.1: Appeal to human cognitive limitations (sceptical theism) as an attempted total refutation.
- §7: Theodicies: soul-making (Hick 1966/1977, with a quotation from Hick 1977 at 255–6); free will; Swinburne's freedom to do great evil; natural laws (Reichenbach 1976); religious theodicies. Objections listed by Tooley (animal pain, children, natural evil, amount).
- Moral vs natural evil: the entry uses "natural evil" (e.g. §1.3, §7.2: evils "caused by natural processes, such as earthquakes, hurricanes ... diseases") and "moral evil".
