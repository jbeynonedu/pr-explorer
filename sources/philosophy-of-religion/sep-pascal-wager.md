## EXACT (lead and §1, §5)
- "“Pascal’s Wager” is the name given to an argument due to Blaise Pascal for believing, or for at least taking steps to believe, in God."
- "in a single section of his Pensées, Pascal apparently presents four such arguments, each of which might be called a ‘wager’—it is only the third of these that is traditionally referred to as “Pascal’s Wager”." (lead)
- "He aims to show that we ought to believe in God, rather than that God exists. And he seeks to provide prudential rather than evidential reasons for believing in God." (§1; italics lost in the PDF text)
- Many Gods objection (§5.1): "If Pascal is really right that reason can decide nothing here, then it would seem that various other theistic hypotheses are also live options." ... "As Diderot (1746) puts the point: “An Imam could reason just as well this way”."
- "One could then concede that practical rationality requires you to maximize expected utility, while insisting that theoretical rationality might require something else of you—say, proportioning belief to the amount of evidence available." (§5.3)

## SUMMARY
- Lead: the Wager draws together the justification of theism, probability and decision theory (used "here for almost the first time in history"), pragmatism, voluntarism (belief as a matter of the will) and infinity.
- §4: The standard reconstruction: a decision table (God exists / does not; wager for / against), infinite utility of salvation, positive probability for God's existence, and the rule to maximise expected utility.
- §5 objections: different tables for different people; infinite utility suspect; more rows (God may not reward mercenary belief: William James); more columns (many Gods, Diderot, Mackie 1982, Cargile 1966), with the reply that some gods are more probable than others (Schlesinger 1994; Jordan 1994); probability of God's existence (premise 2); whether rationality requires maximising expected utility (premise 3); "mixed strategies" objection; moral objections (§5.5).
- §5.4: Several critics nonetheless concede the argument is valid (Mackie 1982, Rescher 1985, Hacking 1972).
- The involuntary-belief objection and Pascal's reply are in Pascal's own text (`pascal-wager.md`).
