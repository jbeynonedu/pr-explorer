## bland-reason-soundness
Greg Bland, *Reason: A Guide to Argument and Critical Reasoning*, Revised Edition, Oct 2026. CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file `Reason Revised Edition.pdf`; text copied from the Project file by script (whitespace normalised). Page numbers are the book's own ("Reason Page N" markers).

> Sound argument – a deductive argument is sound if and only if it is valid and has true premises. (p. 18)

> When it comes to supporting a conclusion in an argument, soundness is the absolute gold standard – the perfect guarantee of a true conclusion. If you can show that a deductive argument is valid (that is, the conclusion is guaranteed by the premises) and you can also show that those premises are factually true, then the truth of the conclusion is guaranteed. (p. 19)

> Now that we’ve explored various types of deductive arguments and developed tools for testing their validity, it’s time to turn to the second key evaluative criterion: soundness. As you may recall from Chapter 2, a sound argument is a deductive argument that is both valid and has all true premises — which means its conclusion must also be true. (p. 66)

> At the end of the day, your task is to consider the available evidence both for and against the truth of a given premise, and to make a reasoned judgement about whether the premise is, in fact, true. In this case, there are likely enough serious objections and counterexamples raised by economists and researchers to prevent us from confidently accepting Premise 1 as true. Since a sound argument requires all of its premises to be established as true, and the weight of evidence does not support Premise 1, the argument fails the standard for soundness. We can therefore say that the argument is valid but unsound. (p. 67)

(The minimum-wage example; not used, per brief.)

> Just remember: when you assess soundness, you need to be decisive. An argument can’t be “partially sound” or “more sound” than another — in the black-and-white world of deductive logic, an argument is either sound or unsound. (p. 67)

> In strict logic, soundness requires that premises are in fact true. In practice, however, we can rarely establish truth with complete certainty — most substantive premises are contested to some degree. Philosophers get around this by adopting a practical standard: we assess whether a premise is plausible — that is, whether it has the appearance of being true — and then we examine whether the weight of evidence supports it. A premise that is both prima facie plausible and well-supported by evidence can be accepted as true for the purposes of a soundness verdict. A premise that fails on either count cannot. This is not a lowering of the standard of soundness; it is an honest acknowledgement that our access to truth is imperfect, and that argument evaluation must proceed on the best evidence available. (p. 67)

> A strong evaluation of a contested premise follows a predictable four-step structure: 1. State the premise clearly and identify why it is contested. 2. Present the case for the premise. What arguments, evidence, or authoritative positions support it? This is where you demonstrate your knowledge of the philosophical theory. 3. Present the case against the premise. What counter-arguments, contrary evidence, or opposing positions challenge it? Cite specific theories or thinkers. 4. Weigh the evidence and make a decisive judgement. On balance, does the weight of evidence support the premise? Be decisive: your verdict is that the premise is true, or that it is not adequately supported to be true. (p. 67)

> f) If the conclusion of a deductive argument is true, does this guarantee that the argument is sound? Explain why/why not. (p. 68)

> The logic in this argument is water-tight – the truth of the premises guarantees the conclusion, so we can say that the argument is valid. However, look at premise 2. Premise 2 consists of a claim that is not supported by any evidence that we can see. Perhaps the person putting forward the argument gave some reasons why Riverview College is a great school, but we just didn’t include these in our standard-form argument. In any case, this premise appears to itself be an end result of inductive reasoning (see the next section for examples of this). Even though the overall deductive argument may contain water-tight, deductive reasoning, one or more of the premises may be subject to challenge because they have been derived by following an inductive process. In your evaluation of such arguments, you can and should consider all the available evidence to determine whether these premises are true or false. (p. 19)

> When we use an argument to prove that its conclusion is true, then, we need two things. First, we need the argument to be valid; i.e., we need the conclusion to follow from the premises. But we also need the premises to be true. We will say that an argument is sound if and only if it is both valid and all of its premises are true. (ch. 2 §2.4 'Sound arguments')

> The flip side of this is that when you want to rebut an argument, you have two options: you can show that (one or more of) the premises are not true, or you can show that the argument is not valid. Logic, however, will only help you with the latter! (ch. 2 §2.4)

True conclusion does not make an argument sound (or valid): London/Beijing/Paris example, valid pack.
