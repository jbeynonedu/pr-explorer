# Source pack: deductive-vs-inductive

## bland-reason-1-4
Greg Bland, *Reason: A Guide to Argument and Critical Reasoning*, Revised Edition, Oct 2026. CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file `Reason Revised Edition.pdf`; text copied from the Project file by script (whitespace normalised). Page numbers are the book's own ("Reason Page N" markers).

> Rather than attempt to guarantee the truth of a conclusion definitively, an inductive argument aims to provide probable support for the conclusion. This is the fundamental distinction between the two argument types: a deductive argument seeks to guarantee its conclusion — if the premises are true, the conclusion must also be true — whereas an inductive argument seeks only to make its conclusion probable. Because of this difference, criteria such as validity and soundness do not apply to inductive arguments — rather, these arguments can be assessed as being either strong or weak overall. Unlike black-and-white deductive arguments, inductive arguments can be thought of as having shades of grey. It is perfectly acceptable to say that one inductive argument is stronger or weaker than another inductive argument — ultimately this judgment will depend on how well the premises provide support for accepting the conclusion (p. 19)

(Footnote marker 3 follows "conclusion" in the source.)

> Validity and soundness are all-or-nothing properties. An argument cannot be partially valid or sound, nor can one argument be more valid or more sound than another. An argument is either valid or it's not. If the argument is valid, then test for soundness — at this point, the argument is either sound or it's not. There are no grey areas. (p. 19)

> What unites all of these forms, however, is the probabilistic nature of the inferential link between the premises and conclusion: regardless of the specific form, the premises of an inductive argument support its conclusion without guaranteeing it. (p. 20)

> Notice that the conclusion uses the word probably. This is the hallmark of an inductive argument. (p. 20)

**Flag:** "hallmark" overstates; IEP (below) says indicator words are not reliable. The entry treats "probably" as a cue, not a test (brief item 5).

> However, it’s not that simple. To build our store of knowledge in the real world, we need to rely heavily on inductive reasoning. In fact, the whole of science is based on the scientific method, which involves developing a hypothesis and then testing this hypothesis using repeated experimental observations. In contrast, the field of mathematics primarily involves deductive reasoning. (p. 21)

> In summary, inductive reasoning can support this conclusion to the extent that it would be accepted under any scientific standard or convention, but it can’t prove the truth of the conclusion. (p. 22)

Footnote 3 (pp. 19–20), split across a box and a page break in the source; the three parts are copied separately:
> It is worth acknowledging that the precise definition of "inductive argument" is not universally agreed upon across the logic and philosophy literature. Some perspectives, particularly those informed by more traditional or schema-based treatments of induction, restrict the term to arguments that conform to one of the recognised inductive patterns – typically generalisation, analogical reasoning, causal inference, and statistical syllogism. Under this narrower view, an argument that does not fit one of these patterns would not strictly be called inductive, even if it provides probabilistic (p. 19)

> rather than certain support for its conclusion. This textbook adopts the broader definition more commonly found in (p. 19)

> modern informal logic and critical thinking texts, under which any argument that aims to provide probabilistic rather than certain support for its conclusion can be classified as inductive. Specific inductive patterns, including the two named in the Philosophy and Reason syllabus (analogy and generalisation), are then understood as particular forms within this broader category and are examined in detail in Chapter 5. (p. 20)

Per brief Q2 default: footnote point left to the Induction entry.

**Flag (Bland footnote 4, p. 22):**
> This actually underlines one valuable aspect of inductive reasoning – even though it may not be possible to prove a statement to be true, if we do find just a single contradiction to this statement then we can happily say that we have disproven the statement. (p. 22)

Refuting a general claim with a single counterexample is a deductive step (modus tollens), not an aspect of inductive reasoning. Do not reproduce this framing.

§9 "Other Even Less Promising Proposals":
- The view (attributed to Kreeft, 2005): deductive arguments are those that begin with "a “general” or “universal” premise"; inductive arguments begin with particular premises and reach a more general conclusion.
- Counterexample 1: "All As are Bs. / All Bs are Cs. / Therefore, all As are Cs." IEP says that on the criticised view it "would be neither deductive nor inductive" (checker); it is in fact valid. Involves "*only* universal statements."
- Counterexample 2: "Each spider so far examined has had eight legs. / Therefore, likewise, the next spider examined will have eight legs." Inductive, but moves from particulars to a particular.
- Verdict: the approach "seems much too crude"; it "faces such *prima facie* plausible exceptions."

§2 and §4 on indicator words:
- "[W]hile indicator words or phrases may suggest specific interpretations, they need to be viewed in context."
- Indicator words "are far from infallible guides".
- "One cannot strictly tell from these indicator words alone." (§4)

Supports brief item 5 (John approved, 25 Nov 2026). An extra counterexample worth using: forall x's Calgary snow argument (below) is inductive and runs from particular past cases to a particular future case.

> Many good arguments are invalid. Consider this one: (ch. 2 §2.5, p. 16)

Example (ch. 2 §2.5, p. 16): "Every winter so far, it has snowed in Calgary. ∴ It will snow in Calgary this coming winter."
> The point of all this is that inductive arguments—even good inductive arguments—are not (deductively) valid. They are not watertight. Unlikely though it might be, it is possible for their conclusion to be false, even when all of their premises are true. In this book, we will set aside (entirely) the question of what makes for a good inductive argument. Our interest is simply in sorting the (deductively) valid arguments from the invalid ones. (ch. 2 §2.5, p. 16)
