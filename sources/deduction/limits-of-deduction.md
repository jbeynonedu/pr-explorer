## bland-reason-limits
Greg Bland, *Reason: A Guide to Argument and Critical Reasoning*, Revised Edition, Oct 2026. CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file `Reason Revised Edition.pdf`; text copied from the Project file by script (whitespace normalised). Page numbers are the book's own ("Reason Page N" markers).

Validity is not truth:
> The premises in this argument are clearly ridiculous – scientific observations have demonstrated that premise 2 is false, and there is certainly no causal link between the composition of celestial bodies and the existence of aliens (premise 1), at least asfar as we know. However, this deductive argument is still valid – if the premises were true, then the conclusion is inescapable. (p. 18)

Premises often come from induction:
> The logic in this argument is water-tight – the truth of the premises guarantees the conclusion, so we can say that the argument is valid. However, look at premise 2. Premise 2 consists of a claim that is not supported by any evidence that we can see. Perhaps the person putting forward the argument gave some reasons why Riverview College is a great school, but we just didn’t include these in our standard-form argument. In any case, this premise appears to itself be an end result of inductive reasoning (see the next section for examples of this). Even though the overall deductive argument may contain water-tight, deductive reasoning, one or more of the premises may be subject to challenge because they have been derived by following an inductive process. In your evaluation of such arguments, you can and should consider all the available evidence to determine whether these premises are true or false. (p. 19)

No new information:
> The important thing to note here is that the intermediate conclusions are drawn purely from the premises in the argument – they add no additional new information. (p. 15)

Why this matters:
> However, it’s not that simple. To build our store of knowledge in the real world, we need to rely heavily on inductive reasoning. In fact, the whole of science is based on the scientific method, which involves developing a hypothesis and then testing this hypothesis using repeated experimental observations. In contrast, the field of mathematics primarily involves deductive reasoning. (p. 21)

> Arguments in this sense are used all the time in everyday and scientific discourse. When they are, arguments are given to support or even prove their conclusions. Now, if an argument is valid, it will support its conclusion, but only if its premises are all true. Validity rules out the possibility that the premises are true and the conclusion is not true at the same time. It does not, by itself, rule out the possibility that the conclusion is not true, period. In other words, it is perfectly possibly for a valid argument to have a conclusion that isn’t true! (ch. 2 §2.4, p. 14)

> The flip side of this is that when you want to rebut an argument, you have two options: you can show that (one or more of) the premises are not true, or you can show that the argument is not valid. Logic, however, will only help you with the latter! (ch. 2 §2.4, p. 15)

(Supports "validity is not truth" and "logic alone cannot settle whether premises are true".)

Example used in the entry (ch. 2 §2.4, p. 14): "Oranges are either fruit or musical instruments. Oranges are not fruit. ∴ Oranges are musical instruments." Replaces the moon/aliens example (checker: the truth of "aliens exist" is unknown).

The Bland Riverview example is rewritten with "our local high school" (Riverview is a real school).

## No-new-information gloss
Brief item 3 says a deductive conclusion can still be informative to the reasoner (e.g. a mathematical proof). Bland's Pythagoras proof (pp. 21–23) shows a result not obvious from the premises being drawn from them deductively:
> What we end up with is an argument so powerful that it proves Pythagoras’ Theorem for any right-angle triangle you could ever think of. (p. 23)

Flag: the phrase "adds no new information" (Bland p. 15) is about intermediate conclusions; extending it to deductive conclusions generally is the entry's own claim, consistent with the approved `conclusion` entry. Checker to confirm wording does not overstate.
