## bland-reason-ch1-deduction
Greg Bland, *Reason: A Guide to Argument and Critical Reasoning*, Revised Edition, Oct 2026. CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file `Reason Revised Edition.pdf`; text copied from the Project file by script (whitespace normalised). Page numbers are the book's own ("Reason Page N" markers).

> Deductive arguments are incredibly powerful when used correctly. Through a sequence of logical steps, a deductive argument aims to prove the truth of the conclusion beyond any doubt whatsoever. One important characteristic of a deductive argument that sets it apart from other argument types is that the premises of the argument must be linked through a logical process to support the conclusion. Each premise must contribute in some way. (p. 17)

> It’s important to note that the validity of a deductive argument has nothing to do with the factual content that is presented in the argument. Validity is only concerned with the structure of the argument. (p. 18)

> However, it’s not that simple. To build our store of knowledge in the real world, we need to rely heavily on inductive reasoning. In fact, the whole of science is based on the scientific method, which involves developing a hypothesis and then testing this hypothesis using repeated experimental observations. In contrast, the field of mathematics primarily involves deductive reasoning. (p. 21)

> What we end up with is an argument so powerful that it proves Pythagoras’ Theorem for any right-angle triangle you could ever think of. (p. 23)

> Although deductive logic traces back to the Greek philosopher Aristotle in his work Organon (p. 25)

> This is the fundamental distinction between the two argument types: a deductive argument seeks to guarantee its conclusion — if the premises are true, the conclusion must also be true — whereas an inductive argument seeks only to make its conclusion probable. (p. 19)

> Inductive arguments take many different forms. Some involve reasoning from observations of specific cases to a more general conclusion, while others involve drawing conclusions about new cases by analogy with familiar ones. We will examine the two most important inductive argument types — arguments from analogy and arguments by generalisation — in detail in Chapter 5. (p. 20)

Note: Bland defines a deductive argument by its **aim** ("aims to prove", p. 17; "seeks to guarantee", p. 19). John's decision (25 Nov 2026): define by aim, with one sentence on the success-based view.

> The point of all this is that inductive arguments—even good inductive arguments—are not (deductively) valid. They are not watertight. Unlikely though it might be, it is possible for their conclusion to be false, even when all of their premises are true. In this book, we will set aside (entirely) the question of what makes for a good inductive argument. Our interest is simply in sorting the (deductively) valid arguments from the invalid ones. (ch. 2 §2.5 'Inductive arguments', p. 16)

## forallx-ch2-validity-by-meaning
forall x: Calgary (details as above), ch. 2. Checked by script against the Fall 2025 PDF.

Example (§2.2, p. 11): "Priya is an ophthalmologist. ∴ Priya is an eye doctor."
> If we’re allowing only conceivable scenarios, this is also a valid argument. If you imagine Priya being an ophthalmologist, you thereby imagine Priya being an eye doctor. That’s just what “ophthalmologist” and “eye doctor” mean. A scenario where Priya is an ophthalmologist but not an eye doctor is ruled out by the conceptual connection between these words. (ch. 2 §2.2, p. 11)

Examples (§2.3 'Formal validity', p. 12): "Priya is either an ophthalmologist or a dentist. Priya isn’t a dentist. ∴ Priya is an eye doctor." and "... ∴ Priya is an ophthalmologist."
> [...] are valid arguments. But while the validity of the first depends on the content (i.e., the meaning of “ophthalmologist” and “eye doctor”), the second does not. The second argument is formally valid. (ch. 2 §2.3, p. 12)

Supports the qualifier (checker, 25 Nov 2026): some arguments are valid because of what their words mean; the arguments studied at this level are valid or invalid because of their form.
