Straw Man
The straw man fallacy can be subtle but devastating. It occurs when somebody replaces an opponent’s real 
argument with a weaker imitation that is easier to attack. Because political debates often involve very complex 
and nuanced topics, you will often see straw man arguments being used when politicians debate each other 
in public, reducing the complex issues to much simpler forms and then attacking these. In the above example, 
Connie makes a claim specifically about the law that bans teenagers from using social media. In response, 
Chloe distorts Connie’s claim into an extreme position (total censorship of the internet and banning teenagers 
from using technological devices) and then responds to this position instead.
The term ‘straw man’ is very literal – historically, straw-filled figures were used for military training, fencing 
practice, and shooting/archery practice. They are shaped like real people but can be knocked over easily and 
offer no real resistance. In modern discourse, straw man arguments are particularly corrosive because they 
are completely contrary to the principle of charity and make it much more difficult to have a genuine debate. 
This makes them not just logically flawed, but intellectually lazy.

[Bland text lines 2875–2881; begins p. 74]
STRAW MAN FALLACY
CONNIE: I think that the federal government’s new law to ban teenagers from using social 
media is reasonable, and will end up reducing harm.
CHLOE: That’s rubbish. Censoring the internet and banning teenagers from using technology will 
just turn us into a nanny state.

## sep-fallacies
Hansen, H., "Fallacies", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 29 May 2015; substantive revision 30 Aug 2024. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fallacies/ (full text fetched via Chrome, 26 Nov 2026).

§1 item 8 (verbatim):
> A variation of ignoratio elenchi, known under the name of the straw man fallacy, occurs when an opponent's point of view is distorted in order to make it easier to refute.
Example (verbatim): "...in opposition to a proponent's view that (a) industrialization is the cause of global warming, an opponent might substitute the proposition that (b) all ills that beset mankind are due to industrialization and then, having easily shown that (b) is false, leave the impression that (a), too, is false. Two things went wrong: the proponent does not hold (b), and even if she did, the falsity of (b) does not imply the falsity of (a)."
§2.5 (Watts, verbatim): "Also, the term ‘strawman fallacy’ may have its origins in Watts's discussion of ignoratio elenchi: after having dressed up the opinions and sentiments of their adversaries as they please to make “images of straw”, disputers “triumph over their adversary as though they had utterly confuted his opinions” (1796, Pt. III 3 i 1)."
§2.3 (Port-Royal, verbatim): ignoratio elenchi extended "to attribut[ing] to our adversaries something remote from their views to gain an advantage over them"
