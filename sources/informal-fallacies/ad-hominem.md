## bland-reason-ch4-adhom
Bland, *Reason* (Revised Edition), ch. 4 §4.1, pp. 70–72. CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file.

Fallacy and informal fallacy (p. 70):
> You may recall that in an earlier chapter, we introduced the concept of a fallacy — a flaw in reasoning that undermines the strength of an argument.
> Informal fallacies, by contrast, are mistakes in content or in the way reasoning is expressed.

Fallacies of relevance (p. 70): "They occur when the premises are not logically relevant to the conclusion, even if they might appear persuasive."

Definition (pp. 70–71):
> This fallacy, usually shortened to ad hominem, occurs when the person making the argument attacks their opponent rather than their opponent's argument.

Abusive (p. 71): presents the Keating example (see Hansard below) as "a clear case of abusive ad hominem, where the speaker merely abuses their opponent rather than attacking their argument." Calls it "probably the most common form of ad hominem attack".
- **Discrepancies with the Hansard record** (do not reproduce Bland's version): Bland says "House of Representatives Question Time in 1991"; the record is the censure-motion debate "GOLD TAX: Motion of Censure", not Question Time. Bland punctuates "You fraud! You disgraceful, disgusting fraud!"; Hansard has full stops. Bland's box omits "Opposition members interjecting-" before "Mr DEPUTY SPEAKER —Order!". Bland calls Keating "then-Treasurer": correct (Hansard: "Mr KEATING (Treasurer)").

Circumstantial (p. 71):
> The circumstantial ad hominem fallacy occurs when an argument is dismissed on the basis of an opponent's circumstances rather than on the merits of their argument itself.

"...this is an issue of ethos, not logos." "...even the most biased, compromised and conflicted individual is still capable of advancing a sound argument, and if we dismiss their argument simply because of their circumstances, we are committing a logical fallacy." (p. 71)
Example box (p. 71): Mr Bland and Jessica ("of course you would say this. You're a maths teacher!"). Not reused (names the author).

Tu quoque (pp. 71–72): "a special kind of circumstantial ad hominem attack, known as tu quoque ... Latin for 'you too'." "the fallacy belongs not to the hypocrite themselves, but to the person calling them out." (Doctor who smokes example.)

Informal fallacies apply to arguments (p. 72, in the ad baculum discussion): "the important difference is that informal fallacies apply to arguments, not merely statements or propositions." Supports brief item 4 (an insult alone is not the fallacy).

Identifying fairly (p. 86): "a fallacy is defined by the quality of the reasoning, not by whether the conclusion is false or unpopular". "interpret arguments charitably before criticising them".

## hansard-1991-04-18-keating
Commonwealth, *Parliamentary Debates*, House of Representatives, Thursday 18 April 1991, p. 2990. "GOLD TAX: Motion of Censure". Speaker: Mr KEATING (Treasurer), 3.58. ParlInfo, system id chamber/hansardr/1991-04-18/0080: https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/search/display/display.w3p;db=CHAMBER;id=chamber/hansardr/1991-04-18/0080 (Chrome, 26 Nov 2026). Context: the Opposition moved censure of the Prime Minister (Hawke) over the gold tax / "WA Inc" questions; Leader of the Opposition Dr Hewson. Keating replies.

Reason given immediately before the abuse (verbatim):
> The Leader of the Opposition then parades himself as a person of integrity. He was impugning the reputation of the Prime Minister while he knew of the position of the honourable member for Pearce. He was happy to stand up here and impugn the reputation of the Prime Minister knowing that one of his own front bench had asked and received $50,000 from the same person.

The abuse (verbatim):
> The Leader of the Opposition has asked these questions, knowing nothing. He knew that the honourable member for Pearce had got $50,000 and he knew of the Bond report, but he asked these questions for two weeks. You fraud. You disgraceful, disgusting fraud.
>
> Opposition members interjecting-
>
> Mr DEPUTY SPEAKER —Order!

Follow-up (verbatim): "Mr KEATING —Of course I will not withdraw them. What about the impropriety? No impropriety was committed or could be established by the Leader of the Opposition."

## sep-fallacies
Hansen, H., "Fallacies", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 29 May 2015; substantive revision 30 Aug 2024. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fallacies/ (Chrome, 26 Nov 2026).

§1, item 13 (verbatim):
> The ad hominem fallacy involves bringing negative aspects of an arguer, or their situation, to bear on the view they are advancing. There are three commonly recognized versions of the fallacy. The abusive ad hominem fallacy involves saying that someone's view should not be accepted because they have some unfavorable property.

> Another, more subtle version of the fallacy is the circumstantial ad hominem in which, given the circumstances in which the arguer finds him or herself, it is alleged that their position is supported by self-interest rather than by good evidence.

> Yet it is possible that the studies are sound: just because what someone says is in their self-interest, does not mean it should be rejected.

Tu quoque: "the value of advice is not wholly dependent on the integrity of the advisor."

§2.4 (Locke): Locke's original *argumentum ad hominem* was "to press a man with consequences drawn from his own principles or concessions", which "is not a fallacy but an acceptable mode of argumentation" (on SEP's account). Modern abusive, circumstantial and tu quoque forms:
> Recent scholarship suggests that these post-Lockean kinds of ad hominem arguments are sometimes used fairly, and sometimes fallaciously; but none of them is what Locke described as the argumentum ad hominem.

Locke: *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690), Bk IV, xvii, 19–22 (per SEP). Possible one-line history note; not required.

Formal vs informal (intro): "Formal fallacies are those readily seen to be instances of identifiable invalid logical forms such as undistributed middle and denying the antecedent." Working definition (§1): "a fallacy to be an argument that seems to be better than it really is." (For the overview entry.)

## iep-fallacies
Dowden, B., "Fallacies", *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, https://iep.utm.edu/fallacy/ (Chrome, 26 Nov 2026). No date shown on page.

"Ad Hominem" (verbatim):
> Your reasoning contains this fallacy if you make an irrelevant attack on the person arguing and suggest that this attack undermines the argument itself.

> The major difficulty with labeling a piece of reasoning an Ad Hominem Fallacy is deciding whether the personal attack is relevant or irrelevant.

Relevance depends on what is being decided: an attack on conduct is irrelevant to someone's reasoning about astronomy but relevant to arguments promoting them for a leadership position (IEP's example). Circumstantial = "irrelevant circumstances", e.g. a vested interest; Abusive = "some despicable trait of the arguer". Tu quoque: "Discovering that a speaker is a hypocrite is a reason to be suspicious of the speaker's reasoning, but it is not a sufficient reason to discount it."
