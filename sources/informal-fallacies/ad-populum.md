Appeal to Popularity (Argumentum Ad Populum)S
There is a phrase that has sentimental meaning to the author – “A nightingale wins no prizes at the chook 
show.” At its heart, it means that there is a disconnect between popularity and quality. In the course of making 
an argument, when somebody invokes the popularity of a concept or idea as logical support for that concept 
or idea, they are committing the ad populum fallacy. History is littered with occasions where terrible ideas 
have enjoyed popular support. For many centuries, slavery was widely defended as morally acceptable and 
was supported by the majority of individuals in many countries. Bloodletting as a medical treatment was the 
go-to option for medieval doctors. And even as recently as the mid-20th-century, the McCarthy-era anticommunist hysteria enjoyed mass support and led to serious injustices. John Stuart Mill made much the same 
point in On Liberty (1859): the popularity of an opinion is no proof of its truth, since every past generation 
has held numerous beliefs now known to be mistaken.

## sep-fallacies
Hansen, H., "Fallacies", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 29 May 2015; substantive revision 30 Aug 2024. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fallacies/ (full text fetched via Chrome, 26 Nov 2026).

§1 item 10 (verbatim):
> The fallacy ad populum is similar to the ad verecundiam, the difference being that the source appealed to is popular opinion, or common knowledge, rather than a specified authority.

Example (verbatim): "These days everyone (except you) has a car and knows how to drive; / So, you too should have a car and know how to drive."
> Often in arguments like this the premises aren't true, but even if they are generally true they may provide only scant support for their conclusions because that something is widely practised or believed is not compelling evidence that it is true or that it should be done.
> There are few subjects on which the general public can be said to hold authoritative opinions.
Also: "playing to the gallery" is described as another version (arousing prejudices and emotions in lieu of evidence).

## iep-fallacies

- "Appeal to the People" (Ad Populum redirects here). EXACT: "However, the fallacy occurs when this degree of support is overestimated."
- SUMMARY: popular belief is somewhat likely to be true, so citing it is reasonable when its support is not overestimated.
- "Bandwagon". SUMMARY: treating a claim as correct only because many people are newly adopting it; citing many people's reasoned study of a claim, rather than mere popularity, is not fallacious.
