4.4 Fallacies of Ambiguity/Misattribution
Not all fallacies arise from bad evidence or bad relevance – some simply arise from bad language. In fallacies 
of ambiguity or misattribution, the error lies not in what is being argued, but in how meanings or properties 
are attributed within the language being used. In this section, we will examine three common ways in which 
ambiguity and misattribution undermine reasoning: ambiguity in word meaning, ambiguity in sentence 
structure, and the mistaken attribution of properties from parts to wholes or from wholes to parts.
CompositionS
The Gestalt principle argues that the whole is other than the sum of its parts. This fascinating idea, originating 
in early 20th century German psychology, concerns the way that humans perceive and organise information. 
It also neatly explains the faulty reasoning behind the composition fallacy – just because the individual 
components of object X have a certain property, it doesn’t mean that object X as a whole has that same 
property as well. The fallacy of composition occurs when it is assumed that what is true of the parts of 
something must also be true of the whole. The example provided above could be true of many high-profile 
sporting teams – just because you put together a team of champions, doesn’t necessarily mean that you will 
have a champion team. The inference fails because not all properties are additive or preserved at the 
collective level.
Clearly, when properties are additive, then there is no fallacy. For example, if you were to have 10 people, 
each weighing 100 kg, just about to step into an elevator with a weight limit of 800 kg, you could quite 
reasonably conclude that the elevator is about to fail.
DivisionS
Just as there is no guarantee that properties of the parts transfer to the whole, there is also no guarantee 
that properties of the whole transfer to the parts. The fallacy of division is simply the opposite of composition 
– it is a misattribution of properties from the group to the individual. As far as fallacies go, division is not as 
egregious or bad-faith as other fallacies like ad hominem or slippery slope. After all, properties of a group 
often do give us some information about its members, and in the example above, it is possible, even likely, 
that Baldacci’s latest novel will be excellent, given his track record as an author and the overall quality of his 
body of work. In fact, if you tweaked the statement to read: “Given his skill and track record, the next Baldacci 
novel is likely to be a good read,” there would be no fallacy. For the fallacy of division, the error occurs when 
the inference is treated as conclusive rather than tentative, and when the speaker ignores the possibility of 
variance among members of the group.
COMPOSITION:
Coach: We have signed the most brilliant, skilful and exciting players this season, so our team is 
sure to succeed!
DIVISION:
Customer: I’m sure that David Baldacci’s latest novel is excellent. He is an outstanding author 
who writes bestselling books.

Lexical Ambiguity (Equivocation)
Fallacies of ambiguity do not typically affect the structure of an entire argument; they are more concerned 
with the clarity and interpretation of individual premises. The first of these, lexical ambiguity, occurs when a 
word has multiple meanings, which opens up the possibility of using different meanings in different parts of 
an argument.
Syntactic Ambiguity (Amphiboly)
Sometimes, uncertainty arises not because words have multiple meanings, but because the structure of a 
sentence admits multiple different interpretations. These cases come under the category of syntactic 
ambiguity, and can have humorous outcomes, as you can see in the above example.

## sep-fallacies

§1 items 3 & 4 (verbatim):
> The fallacies of composition and division occur when the properties of parts and composites are mistakenly thought to be transferable from one to the other.

Example (verbatim): "Every member of the investigative team was an excellent researcher. / It was an excellent investigative team."
> The fallacy of composition is the inference from (a) to (b) but it need not hold if members of the team cannot work cooperatively with each other. The reverse inference from (b) to (a)—the fallacy of division—may also fail if some essential members of the team have a supportive or administrative role rather than a research role.
