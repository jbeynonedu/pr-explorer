False CauseS
Causation is one of the deepest and most contested topics in philosophy. Sitting at the intersection of 
metaphysics, epistemology and science, the topic has occupied the attention of philosophers such as 
Aristotle, Hume, Kant, and Mill. From your experience of the real world, you may believe it is a relatively 
simple statement to say that action X causes outcome Y, but in fact, philosophers not only disagree about 
which things cause which, but also about what causation even is. The nuances of this topic are beyond the 
scope of this book, but suffice to say that philosophers have long recognised that causation is not directly 
observable but inferred, making causal reasoning especially vulnerable to error.
The false cause19 fallacy occurs when one infers that event A causes event B on insufficient grounds – either 
because B happened to follow A in time, or simply because A and B are correlated with one another. 
Statisticians are especially careful when it comes to this fallacy – in any basic course in statistics or research 
methodology, students are presented ad nauseam with the maxim: correlation does not imply causation. 
Just because two things occur one after the other, or merely tend to appear together, it doesn't mean that 
any causal relationship exists between them. Take Bruce's reasoning in the above example – he seems to 
think that his lucky underpants somehow cause his favourite NRL team to win their games, because he 
happened to be wearing the underwear during past wins. (One hopes that he does not extend his faulty 
reasoning even further by refusing to wash said underwear). The two things have nothing to do with each 
other, hence the false cause fallacy is being committed.
The real world is littered with interesting examples of faulty causal reasoning. Have you ever heard the myth 
that storks deliver babies? This unlikely association gathered some seriously spurious evidence in the late 19th
and early 20th centuries in Germany and the Netherlands, where it was observed that there was a measurable 
correlation between declining stork populations and declining human birth rates. Evidence that storks bring 
babies? Hardly… the more boring reality was that increased industrialisation reduced rural living, which at 
once lowered human birth rates (smaller families in cities) and destroyed stork habitats (they nested mostly 
on farm buildings). Improvements in housing and sanitation further discouraged storks, while social changes 
such as education and contraception further reduced fertility.
19 Although the label 'false cause' is a perfectly acceptable language choice in argument evaluation, the true fallacy 
enthusiast will instead appreciate the intellectual gravitas bestowed by using one of the Latin names for the two 
subspecies of this fallacy type – post hoc ergo propter hoc (after this, therefore because of this) and cum hoc ergo propter 
hoc (with this, therefore because of this). The first of these, post hoc, occurs when there is a temporal sequence: event 
B happens after event A, and A is therefore assumed to be the cause of B. The second, cum hoc, occurs when two things 
are merely correlated – they tend to occur together – and one is therefore assumed to cause the other, when the 
association may be coincidental or the product of a common underlying cause. Strictly speaking, Bruce’s fallacious 
underpants reasoning in the above example is a case of cum hoc ergo propter hoc, because he has picked up on a 
correlation rather than just a simple series of events over time. The syllabus uses the terms 'false cause' and 'post hoc 
ergo propter hoc' interchangeably; strictly, though, they are not identical.

## sep-fallacies
Hansen, H., "Fallacies", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 29 May 2015; substantive revision 30 Aug 2024. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fallacies/ (full text fetched via Chrome, 26 Nov 2026).

§1 item 7 (verbatim):
> There are a number of fallacies associated with causation, the most frequently discussed is post hoc ergo propter hoc, (after this, therefore because of this). This fallacy ascribes a causal relationship between two states or events on the basis of temporal succession.

Example (verbatim): "Unemployment decreased in the fourth quarter because the government eliminated the gasoline tax in the second quarter."
> The decrease in unemployment that took place after the elimination of the tax may have been due to other causes; perhaps new industrial machinery or increased international demand for products. Other fallacies involve confusing the cause and the effect, and overlooking the possibility that two events are not directly related to each other but are both the effect of a third factor, a common cause. These fallacies are perhaps better understood as faults of explanation than faults of arguments.

§2.3 (Port-Royal Logic, verbatim): "Also identified as a common fallacy of the human mind is post hoc, ergo propter hoc: “This happened following a certain thing, hence that thing must be its cause” (Bk. III, xix 3)."
§2.5 (Watts): "false cause" introduced as an alternative name for *non causa pro causa*.
