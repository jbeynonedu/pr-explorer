Appeal to Inappropriate Authority (Argumentum Ad Verecundiam)S
Have you ever watched TV and noticed an advertisement where a professional athlete is recommending a 
certain brand of air conditioner? Have you ever scrolled through your socials to find that a celebrity actor is 
endorsing a fragrance or beauty product? If so, you have probably experienced just how effective an ad 
verecundiam fallacy can actually be in persuading you to accept a certain point of view. In an argument, it is 
relatively common to invoke the perspectives of other people when attempting to support a conclusion. 
However, when the individuals you choose to support your position have absolutely no expert knowledge, 
then you are committing an ad verecundiam fallacy – an illicit appeal to inappropriate authority.
At this point, the perceptive reader may well ask… “But if I include the testimony or opinion of anybody in my 
argument, aren’t I drifting from the world of logos into the world of ethos? And aren’t we supposed to limit 
ourselves to logos when constructing an argument?” This is an entirely reasonable question, and if this 
thought crossed your mind, congratulations – you have really been paying attention! Logical arguments 
involving complex concepts will often require specific, detailed, and technical information, and the inclusion 
of genuinely expert authority can play a legitimate epistemic role in argument. In real-world contexts, we 
simply lack the time or expertise to verify everything ourselves, and we have no choice but to rely on people 
who have amassed expertise in an area through experience or scholarly study. Law courts could not function, 
for example, without the benefit of expert testimony. But caution must be exercised when constructing an 
argument that relies on the expertise of others. When used properly, the reasoning looks like this:
1. This person is an expert in domain D.
2. The claim C falls squarely within domain D.
3. There is broad agreement among experts in D regarding C.
C. Therefore, C is likely true.
This argument is not deductively valid, but it is a reasonable inductive inference. The authority does not 
replace reasons – it stands in for inaccessible reasons. An argument becomes an ad verecundiam fallacy when

the authority is treated as a substitute for reasoning rather than as a guide to it. The clearest examples of ad 
verecundiam fallacies occur when the individual being cited has no expertise whatsoever in the claim that is 
being put forward. Celebrity endorsements are the most obvious examples of this, but any situation where 
an individual is offering opinions beyond their sphere of expertise is also an example of ad verecundiam.

## sep-fallacies
Hansen, H., "Fallacies", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 29 May 2015; substantive revision 30 Aug 2024. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fallacies/ (full text fetched via Chrome, 26 Nov 2026).

§1 item 9 (verbatim):
> The ad verecundiam fallacy concerns appeals to authority or expertise. Fundamentally, the fallacy involves accepting as evidence for a proposition the pronouncement of someone who is taken to be an authority but is either not really an authority or a relevant authority. This can happen when non-experts parade as experts in fields in which they have no special competence—when, for example, celebrities endorse commercial products or social movements. Similarly, when there is controversy, and authorities are divided, it is an error to base one's view on the authority of just some of them.

§2.4 (verbatim): "It seems unlikely, however, that Locke thought we should never rely on the expertise and superior knowledge of others when engaged in knowledge-gathering and argumentation." Also: "Hence, we understand Locke better when we translate ad verecundiam literally, as “appeal to modesty.”"
