Begging the Question (Petitio Principii)S
When constructing an argument, perhaps the most important structural consideration is that the premises 
must not presuppose the truth of the conclusion. If this happens, then the argument becomes circular and 
completely breaks down. The petitio principii fallacy involves this very mistake, although it isn’t always easy 
to identify. Sometimes the person making the argument may kind of ‘slip in’ the conclusion amidst the 
premises, often using slightly different wording. When set out in standard form, the fallacy goes like this:
1. Claim P is true (stated directly or indirectly).
C. Therefore, P is true.
To identify this fallacy, it might be helpful to ask yourself the following question: If I didn’t already accept the 
conclusion, would the premises give me any reason to accept it? If the answer is no, then the argument likely 
begs the question.
PETITIO PRINCIPII

## sep-fallacies

§1 item 5 (verbatim):
> The fallacy of begging the question (petitio principii) can occur in a number of ways.
> This argument begs the question because the premise and conclusion are the very same proposition, albeit expressed in different words. It is a disguised instance of repetition which gives no reason for its apparent conclusion.
(The example is Whately's, on unbounded freedom of speech.)
> Another version of begging the question can occur in contexts of argumentation where there are unsettled questions about key terms.
(Example: capital punishment as "murder"; politically live: not reused.)
> Neither of these versions of begging the question are faulted for their invalidity, so they are not charged with being non-sequitors like most of the core fallacies
> But, given our allegiance to the modern conception of logic as being solely concerned with the following-from relation, forms of begging the question should be thought of as epistemic rather than logical fallacies.
> Some versions of begging the question are more involved and are called circular reasoning. They include more than one inference.
(Descartes's example on the Bible and God: religious content, not reused.)

## iep-fallacies

- SUMMARY (not fallacious): if a key premise can be defended independently of the conclusion, the argument is not question-begging.
- EXACT (IEP introduction, search snippet): "Reasoning validly is not a guarantee of avoiding a fallacy since begging the question is valid."
- "Circular Reasoning" SUMMARY: short circles are fallacious; large, informative circles (like a dictionary) are not.
