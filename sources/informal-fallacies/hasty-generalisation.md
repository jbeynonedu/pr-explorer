4.2 Fallacies of Weak Induction
In the section we have just considered, Fallacies of Relevance, usually the argumentative offence is clear and 
easy to identify. Fallacies of weak induction, however, are not as straightforward. The premises are relevant 
and the reasoning feels… reasonable. However, the argument still fails, not because the premises are 
irrelevant, but because they are insufficient.
Hasty GeneralisationS
In Chapter 5, we will consider inductive arguments, and their subtypes, in much greater detail. However, for 
the purposes of this explanation, I’m sure that you will understand what it means to make a generalisation. 
As humans, for better or for worse, we make generalisations all the time. It is one way that we make sense of 
the complexity in the world around us. And indeed, these generalisations can be incredibly useful to us. For 
example, it doesn’t require much empirical evidence for us to arrive at the conclusion that “snakes are 
dangerous.” It’s a generalisation, sure, and it may not even be particularly accurate, but it is useful for us in a 
biological and evolutionary sense – when we see a snake, most of us sensibly stay some distance away.
However, as useful and essential as these generalisations might be to our basic survival, when used in more 
complex social scenarios, they can lead to gross inaccuracies in reasoning, not to mention the development 
of harmful, long-term prejudices. Let’s face it – I’m sure you have heard phrases like “teenagers are 
irresponsible”, “lawyers are dishonest”, “welfare recipients are lazy”, and – heaven forfend – “maths teachers 
are nerds.” These are stereotypes – generalised beliefs about groups of people that are formed on the basis 
of limited, selected, or unrepresentative evidence.
When constructing arguments, it is especially important to take care when adducing qualities or 
characteristics of a group based on individual members. A hasty generalisation fallacy occurs when a broad 

[p. 77]
conclusion is drawn from a sample that is too small, unrepresentative, or biased to justify that conclusion.
The fallacy has the following basic structure:
1. Some members of group A have property B.
C. Therefore, all or most members of group A have property B.
Clearly, this fallacy lies in overreach, not irrelevance. To be categorised as a hasty generalisation, two elements 
are essential. Firstly, the argument needs to be inductive – it needs to move from specific cases to a general 
claim. Secondly, the evidence needs to be insufficient for the strength of the conclusion drawn.
Although hasty generalisations are relatively easy to identify, in the author’s experience they are among the 
most misidentified fallacies by students of Philosophy and Reason. As an example of why this is the case, 
consider the following argument:
1. If a policy results in increased crime, then the policy should be abandoned.
2. Capital punishment results in increased crime.
C. Therefore, capital punishment should be abandoned.
You might correctly identify this argument as a garden variety, deductive modus ponens. However, it is very 
common for students who are evaluating arguments like this to claim, for example, that premise 2 consists of 
a hasty generalisation fallacy, because the premise itself is a kind of generalisation based (presumably) on 
some real-life data from jurisdictions that allow capital punishment. This evaluation is well-intentioned and 
on the right track, but to be clear, it is not an example of hasty generalisation. Premise 2 is simply a claim; it 
would certainly be worthwhile to interrogate this claim for accuracy and truthfulness, but unless an inference 
is being made in the argument that is based on insufficient cases, then there is no hasty generalisation fallacy.
Contrast this with the following argument:
1. In Texas and Louisiana, capital punishment has led to increased crime.
IC1. Therefore, capital punishment leads to increased crime.
2. If a policy results in increased crime, then the policy should be abandoned.
C. Therefore, capital punishment should be abandoned.
In this argument, the intermediate conclusion IC1 is based on evidence from premise 1. The combination of 
P1 and IC1 represents a hasty generalisation fallacy, because an inference is drawn about a group based on 
only a small number of individual cases. Can you see the difference between the two arguments?

4.5 Identifying Fallacious Reasoning
In the real world, identifying fallacies is messier than the tidy examples in this chapter might suggest –
especially for fallacies of weak induction, where deciding whether the evidence is too thin to support a 
conclusion is often a judgement call. A word of caution, then: students commonly hunt so eagerly for fallacies 
that they start seeing them where none exist. When you evaluate arguments in your analytical essays, there 
is no requirement to name specific fallacies, and it pays to tread carefully.
Two principles will keep you honest. First, a fallacy is defined by the quality of the reasoning, not by whether 
the conclusion is false or unpopular – a true conclusion can rest on poor reasoning, and careful reasoning can 
still reach a false one. Your task is not to decide whether you agree with the claim, but whether the reasons 
genuinely support it. Second, context matters: everyday reasoning often relies on incomplete information and 
background assumptions without being fallacious, and arguments sit on a spectrum of strength rather than 
falling neatly into "strong" and "fallacious" boxes. For this reason, interpret arguments charitably before 
criticising them — ask whether a shaky argument could be reasonably strengthened before you condemn it. 
We will return to this principle of charity in the next chapter.
