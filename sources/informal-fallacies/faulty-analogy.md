Faulty AnalogyS
In the next chapter, we will consider in substantial detail the inductive argument from analogy. Suffice to say 
for now that an argument from analogy is one that draws an inference about one concept or scenario based 
on its similarity to another concept or scenario about which some property is known. For example, consider 
the watchmaker argument – probably the most famous analogical argument in philosophy. This argument 
likens the complexity of the human body to the complexity of a wristwatch, and draws the inference that 
because a wristwatch has an intelligent designer, so too must the human body be the product of an intelligent 
designer. It is essentially an argument in favour of a creator god, and relies on the fact that both a wristwatch 
and a person have something in common – that is, their complexity.
A faulty analogy occurs when an argument relies on an analogy in which the similarities between the two 
things being compared are not sufficiently relevant to justify the conclusion drawn. In the example above, a 
father makes a claim about his capacity to run the country based on an analogy between a government and 
his own household. Although these two things may share some very rudimentary similarities, in reality they 
are enormously different – a country issues currency, levies taxation, and provides for millions of citizens 
whereas a household is limited to a very small number of individuals. Countries and households are just too 
different to allow any reasonable analogy to be made, and any attempt to do so will ultimately end in 
fallacious reasoning.

## sep-fallacies
Hansen, H., "Fallacies", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 29 May 2015; substantive revision 30 Aug 2024. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fallacies/ (full text fetched via Chrome, 26 Nov 2026).

§1 item 14 (verbatim):
> The fallacy of faulty analogy occurs when analogies are used as arguments or explanations and the similarities between the two things compared are too remote to support the conclusion.

Example (verbatim): "If a child gets a new toy he or she will want to play with it; / So, if a nation gets new weapons, it will want to use them." (SEP credits Churchill 1986, 349.) SEP explains the relevant difference: toys are used for amusement, weapons to inflict harm, usually only after extensive deliberation.
