Gambler’s FallacyS
In 1998, Melbourne-based truck driver Bill Morgan was involved in a car accident that almost ended his life. 
He was clinically dead for around 14 minutes and was in a coma for 12 days afterwards. He eventually 
recovered, and later that year, purchased a scratch-it ticket at a news agency. Remarkably, he won a 
substantial amount of money – around $30,000, enough to buy a Toyota Corolla.
This combination of awful and very good luck was amazing enough, but what happened next has become 
internet folklore. When a TV news crew asked him to re-enact that moment on camera, Morgan purchased 
another scratch-it card and, in front of the cameras, scratched the ticket to reveal that he had won the major 
prize - $250,000.17
The fickle nature of probability means that seemingly astonishing wins such as Morgan’s will indeed occur 
from time to time. What you probably don’t hear about are the astonishing losses, or the very vague, boring 
combinations of wins and losses that happen each and every day. The thing about probability is that it is 
inherently unpredictable, and as long as you are dealing with independent events18, you simply cannot infer 
anything about future outcomes based on what has happened in the past.
Say, for example, that you are rolling a fair die. This particular die has 6 sides, each of which has an equal 
chance of facing up when the die is rolled. Let’s say that the last 5 times you have rolled this particular die, 
the outcome has been a 1. What is the probability that you will roll a 1 on the next roll? Well, although it’s a 
very unusual occurrence to roll five 1s in a row, there is no ‘god of probability’ that is looking over your 
shoulder and making it more likely that any other number will be rolled next time. In fact, the probability of 
rolling a 1 on the next roll is exactly the same as it has always been – 1 out of 6. The other numbers are not 
‘overdue’ to be rolled, nor is it more likely that you will roll a 1 next time to continue the streak. Somebody 
who believes that past outcomes affect present probability is suffering from the gambler’s fallacy. It is 
included here in the ‘fallacies of weak induction’ section because the individual committing this fallacy is using 
dodgy statistical reasoning to arrive at a claim.
17 See the video at https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wBYuxQBSc0o.
18 The gambler’s fallacy only applies to situations where the probability events are independent, that is, one event has 
no impact on the subsequent events. Casino games like roulette fall into this category – if you land on a red square on 
one spin, for example, it has no impact on the colour of the next spin. Some games such as blackjack do not fall into this 
category – probabilities are dependent because the cards that are drawn are not replaced. If you see an ace being drawn, 
for example, it means that there is one fewer ace remaining in the shoe (the set of remaining cards). Therefore, it is less 
likely that an ace will be drawn in the next game. Savvy players are able to exploit this knowledge and count the cards
that are dealt, giving themselves a very small advantage over the house. This technique is not illegal… but if a casino 
notices you counting cards and adjusting your bets accordingly, they will likely ask you to leave the premises.

LUCY: I’ve put my lotto entry in tonight. I just know I’m going to win!
AMANDA: Really? How can you be so sure?
LUCY: Well, I have picked numbers 7, 19, 20, 45 and 49. None of these numbers have come up in 
the last ten draws, so it’s almost guaranteed that they will come up this time! They are due for a 
win – it’s just basic probability!
