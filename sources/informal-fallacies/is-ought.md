THE IS-OUGHT PROBLEM
Articulated by 18th Century Scottish philosopher David Hume, the is-ought problem concerns the 
fact that there is a logical gap when trying to argue for a normative conclusion based on descriptive
premises or statements. For example, consider the following argument:
1) Cigarette smoking causes ill health.
C) Therefore, the government should ban cigarette smoking.
Can you see the problem here? Even if the first premise is factually correct, it is a descriptive
proposition that tells you what something is. On the other hand, the conclusion is a normative
proposition which talks about what the government ought to do. This problem can be solved by 
including an additional premise that bridges the gap between the descriptive premise and the 
normative conclusion:
1) Cigarette smoking causes ill health.
2) The government has a duty to protect public health.
C) Therefore, the government should ban cigarette smoking.
Can you see an example of the is-ought problem in Penny’s argument?

> I cannot forbear adding to these reasonings an observation, which may, perhaps, be found of some importance. In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remarked, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surprised to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as this ought, or ought not, expresses some new relation or affirmation, it is necessary that it should be observed and explained; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it. But as authors do not commonly use this precaution, I shall presume to recommend it to the readers; and am persuaded, that this small attention would subvert all the vulgar systems of morality, and let us see, that the distinction of vice and virtue is not founded merely on the relations of objects, nor is perceived by reason.

## sep-hume-moral

- EXACT: "Few passages in Hume's work have generated more interpretive controversy."

- EXACT: "Some see the paragraph as denying ethical realism, excluding values from the domain of facts."
- EXACT: "Other interpreters — the more cognitivist ones — see the paragraph about ‘is’ and ‘ought’ as doing none of the above."
- EXACT: "They point out that Hume himself makes such inferences frequently in his writings."
- SUMMARY: the dominant 20th-century reading takes the passage as a logical rule that evaluative conclusions cannot follow from purely factual premises; cognitivist readers take it to show only that reason alone cannot make the first discovery of moral properties, which requires sentiment.
