Chapter 4 – Informal Fallacies
You may recall that in an earlier chapter, we introduced the concept of a fallacy — a flaw in reasoning that 
undermines the strength of an argument. In our examination of deductive logic, we encountered two wellknown formal fallacies: denying the antecedent and affirming the consequent. These represent errors in the 
structure of an argument, and they render a deductive argument invalid — meaning that the conclusion does 
not logically follow from the premises, regardless of how persuasive it might sound.
Informal fallacies, by contrast, are mistakes in content or in the way reasoning is expressed. They may not 
violate the formal rules of logic, but they can still derail an argument just as effectively. Informal fallacies can 
appear in both inductive and deductive contexts, and identifying them is a critical skill for anyone wishing to 
evaluate arguments with clarity and precision.
Many of these fallacies bear Latin names — a nod to their long-standing place in the tradition of logic and 
philosophy. While the Philosophy and Reason syllabus does not require students to use these Latin terms in 
their evaluations, one cannot deny that expressions like ad hominem or post hoc ergo propter hoc lend a 
certain academic gravitas to a critique. Indeed, ceteris paribus, invoking a Latin label in conversation may 
discourage further questioning from the layperson, and create the impression that one’s knowledge is both 
ancient and unassailable. Accordingly, this book will refer to each fallacy in both English and its traditional 
Latin name, where appropriate.14
At this point, it may be worth highlighting a key distinction between the subject Senior Philosophy and Reason
and other senior subjects such as Senior English. In Senior English, students study the techniques of 
persuasive speech, including the three classical pillars of rhetoric: ethos, pathos, and logos. Each of these 
appeals aims to persuade the audience of the merits of a particular position or theme. Pathos appeals to 
emotion — aiming to persuade through emotions such as fear, excitement, or sympathy. Ethos appeals to the 
character or credibility of the speaker or writer. Logos appeals to reason, and involves the use of evidence, 
logic, and structured argument.
Of these three rhetorical appeals, only logos is relevant to the study of Philosophy and Reason. Our focus is 
on the structure and content of arguments as they relate to truth, rationality, and justification. Appeals to 
emotion (pathos) or to personal authority (ethos) may be effective in persuasive contexts, but they play no 
role in our evaluation of arguments. In fact, such appeals often function as informal fallacies — distractions 
from the real task of constructing and assessing arguments through clear-eyed, critical reasoning.
Here then are the most common informal fallacies, including all of the fallacies explicitly included in the 2025 
Syllabus (marked with a superscript S). Fallacy enthusiasts will note that there are book and website resources 
on this topic that are much more detailed and thorough than the chapter provided here.
4.1 Fallacies of Relevance
Fallacies of relevance are perhaps the most common type of informal fallacy that you will encounter. They 
occur when the premises are not logically relevant to the conclusion, even if they might appear persuasive.

4.5 Identifying Fallacious Reasoning
In the real world, identifying fallacies is messier than the tidy examples in this chapter might suggest –
especially for fallacies of weak induction, where deciding whether the evidence is too thin to support a 
conclusion is often a judgement call. A word of caution, then: students commonly hunt so eagerly for fallacies 
that they start seeing them where none exist. When you evaluate arguments in your analytical essays, there 
is no requirement to name specific fallacies, and it pays to tread carefully.
Two principles will keep you honest. First, a fallacy is defined by the quality of the reasoning, not by whether 
the conclusion is false or unpopular – a true conclusion can rest on poor reasoning, and careful reasoning can 
still reach a false one. Your task is not to decide whether you agree with the claim, but whether the reasons 
genuinely support it. Second, context matters: everyday reasoning often relies on incomplete information and 
background assumptions without being fallacious, and arguments sit on a spectrum of strength rather than 
falling neatly into "strong" and "fallacious" boxes. For this reason, interpret arguments charitably before 
criticising them — ask whether a shaky argument could be reasonably strengthened before you condemn it. 
We will return to this principle of charity in the next chapter.

Although many of the informal fallacies are also invalid arguments, it is generally thought to be more profitable, from the points of view of both recognition and understanding, to bring their weaknesses to light through analyses that do not involve appeal to formal languages.
