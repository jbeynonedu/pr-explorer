Slippery SlopeS
In reality, when one encounters a slippery slope, it is usually a quick ride to the bottom. Likewise, when putting 
together an argument, it can be easy to lose control. A slippery slope fallacy occurs when it is argued that a 
relatively small or moderate step will inevitably lead to a chain reaction where extreme and undesirable 
consequences follow, and when there is no logical justification for that chain reaction. The key word here is 
‘inevitably’ – the argument assumes momentum where none has been demonstrated.
The fallacy lies not in the causal chain of events, but in the unsupported links between the events. You can 
normally spot a slippery slope fallacy because it begins small and snowballs into something unjustified and 
extreme. It’s a fallacy that you often see in moral debate, where the argument is constructed to exploit fear 
and bypass careful analysis. The example above is typical of the kinds of arguments you encounter in a public 
forum when social reforms are proposed. Importantly, not all arguments that involve chain or consequential 
reasoning are slippery slopes – if the proposed causal links are well supported by evidence, then the argument 
is not fallacious.

## sep-fallacies
Hansen, H., "Fallacies", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 29 May 2015; substantive revision 30 Aug 2024. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fallacies/ (full text fetched via Chrome, 26 Nov 2026).

§1 item 15 (verbatim):
> The fallacy of the slippery slope generally takes the form that from a given starting point one can by a series of incremental inferences arrive at an undesirable conclusion, and because of this unwanted result, the initial starting point should be rejected.

Example (verbatim): "You have decided not to go to college; / If you don't go to college, you won't get a degree; / If you don't get a degree, you won't get a good job; / If you don't get a good job, you won't be able to enjoy life; / But you should be able to enjoy life; / So, you should go to college."
> The weakness in this argument, the reason why it is a fallacy, lies in the second and third causal claims. The series of small steps that lead from an acceptable starting point to an unacceptable conclusion may also depend on vague terms rather than causal relations.
"the beard" and "the heap" are given as vague-term slippery slopes.
