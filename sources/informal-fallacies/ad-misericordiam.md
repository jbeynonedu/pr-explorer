Appeal to Pity (Argumentum Ad Misericordiam)S
Tugging at the heart-strings of the audience may be an effective rhetorical device, but in the course of putting 
forward an argument, if somebody attempts to elicit the sympathy of the audience, they are appealing not 
to logos, but to pathos. Logical reasoning and argumentation demand an impartial, level-headed approach, 
and there is no room for emotion. In the above example, recidivist musician Jake Blues (John Belushi) pleads 
for his life with his enraged ex-fiancée (Carrie Fisher), providing an ever-more ridiculous list of excuses for 
why he left her standing at the altar on their wedding day. His ad misericordiam argument works in this case, 
giving him enough time to dump her once again and flee the scene with his brother Elwood.

## sep-fallacies
Hansen, H., "Fallacies", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 29 May 2015; substantive revision 30 Aug 2024. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fallacies/ (full text fetched via Chrome, 26 Nov 2026).

§1 item 12 (verbatim):
> The fallacy ad misericordiam is a companion to the ad baculum fallacy: it occurs not when threats are out of place but when appeals for sympathy or pity are mistakenly thought to be evidence. To what extent our sympathy for others should influence our actions depends on many factors, including circumstances and our ethical views. However, sympathy alone is generally not evidence for believing any proposition.

Example (verbatim): "You should believe that he is not guilty of embezzling those paintings; think of how much his family suffered during the Depression."
> Ad misericordiam arguments, like ad baculum arguments, have their natural home in practical reasoning; it is when they are used in theoretical (doxastic) argumentation that the possibility of fallacy is more likely.

§2.5 (Watts) adds *argumentum ad passiones* ("appeal to passion").
