# Source pack: modus tollens

## bland-reason-ch2
Bland, *Reason* (Revised Edition), ch. 2, pp. 26–27. CC BY-NC 4.0. Project file.

> Modus tollens (translated: "the method of denying", short for modus tollendo tollens meaning "the method of denying by denying") works a little differently. It sets up a conditional statement then denies the consequent of that conditional, which then denies the conclusion also. (p. 27)

Example (p. 27): "If I win the lotto, then am wealthy. I'm not wealthy, therefore I didn't win the lotto." L = "I win the lotto." W = "I am wealthy." 1. If L, then W. 2. Not W. C. Therefore, not L. "This argument is deductively valid, in that the conclusion is guaranteed by the premises."

Form box (p. 27): MODUS TOLLENS 1. If P, then Q. 2. Not Q. C. Therefore, not P.

Caution box (p. 27): "The modus tollens argument form is valid because it denies the consequent of the conditional. If you encounter an inferential argument that denies the antecedent of a conditional, this argument is invalid."

Antecedent/consequent (p. 26): the proposition after "if" is the antecedent; after "then", the consequent. Equivalent wordings of "If R, then W": "If R, W." "W if R." "R only if W." "R is sufficient for W." "W is necessary for R."

## bland-reason-ch3-truth-table
Bland, ch. 3, pp. 50–51. Symbolised: 1. L ⊃ W 2. ~W C. ~L. Testing rule (p. 50 box): no row with all true premises and a false conclusion → VALID; at least one such row → INVALID (that row is a counterexample). Rows = 2^p. Bland's convention: 1 = true, 0 = false; right-most proposition alternates 1, 0; next 1, 1, 0, 0. Bland's conclusion (p. 51): "When we look at the top two rows, there is no case containing all true premises. Therefore, this argument is valid."

## bland-reason-ch3-wording
Bland, ch. 3, pp. 68–69: "If a person is compassionate, then they donate to charity. Maria does not give money to charity. Therefore, Maria is not compassionate." Appears to be modus tollens but "donate to charity" and "give money to charity" may not mean the same thing, so "the argument is not deductively valid". Footnote 13: generalised version of modus tollens acceptable for the course.

## bland-reason-answers
Bland, exercise answers, p. 121, (i): F = "The car is out of fuel." E = "The engine starts." 1. If F, then not E. 2. E. C. Therefore, not F. (Modus tollens). Shows negated components.
